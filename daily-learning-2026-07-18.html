The Photo That Shouldn’t Have Been Possible: The First Image of a Black Hole (黑洞)

🤔 Predict Before You Read
On 10 April 2019, scientists released the very first photograph of a black hole (黑洞) — an object whose entire defining trait is that it emits no light at all. Before reading on, what do you think a “photograph” of something invisible could possibly show?
M87* (M87黑洞), the first-ever photograph of a black hole, released 10 April 2019. Credit: Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration / Wikimedia Commons

On 10 April 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope (事件視界望遠鏡) collaboration unveiled humanity’s first-ever image of a black hole: a glowing, asymmetric orange ring of superheated gas wrapped around a dark central shadow, roughly the size and shape general relativity (廣義相對論) predicted. The target was M87* (M87黑洞), a supermassive black hole (超大質量黑洞) with a mass of about 6.5 billion Suns, sitting at the centre of the galaxy Messier 87 (M87星系), 55 million light-years away in the constellation Virgo (室女座).

Capturing it required an extraordinary trick: since no single telescope on Earth is large enough to resolve something so distant and small, astronomers linked eight radio observatories spread from Hawaii to the South Pole into a single virtual instrument the size of the planet, using a technique called very-long-baseline interferometry (特長基線干涉測量法). Atomic clocks at each site synchronised the recordings down to fractions of a second, and each telescope generated so much raw data — several petabytes in total — that it had to be recorded onto hundreds of physical hard drives and flown by plane to processing centres, since even modern internet links couldn’t move it fast enough.

Computer scientist Katie Bouman (凱蒂·鮑曼) helped develop one of several independent imaging algorithms used to reconstruct a picture from the incomplete, noisy interferometric data, and four separate teams worked in isolation from each other precisely so their results could be cross-checked without bias. All four converged on essentially the same ring. Three years later, in 2022, the same collaboration released a second image — this time of Sagittarius A* (人馬座A*), the much smaller black hole at the centre of our own Milky Way (銀河系).

「稱病十年，一擊定局」：司馬懿與高平陵之變

事件：249年「高平陵之變」，司馬懿趁曹爽陪同魏帝曹芳出城祭掃高平陵之機，發動政變奪取洛陽控制權。

曹爽掌權後逐步排擠司馬懿，司馬懿遂稱病不朝，甚至在曹爽心腹李勝探病時故意表現昏聵失智——粥水灑滿衣襟，言語顛三倒四，成功令李勝回報「司馬公已是行將就木之人，不足為慮」。曹爽由此對司馬懿完全放下戒心，未料這份「昏聵」，實為長達十年的隱忍蓄勢。

“England Expects”: Nelson’s Gamble at the Battle of Trafalgar (特拉法加海戰), 1805

🤔 Predict Before You Read
A British admiral, facing an enemy fleet larger than his own, abandons the standard naval tactic of lining ships up side-by-side to trade broadsides — a formation used for over a century. Instead he sails two columns of ships directly at the enemy line, exposing his lead ships to unanswered cannon fire during the approach. Before reading on, why might a commander deliberately choose the riskier option?
Context

Key figures: Vice-Admiral Horatio Nelson (霍雷肖·納爾遜), commanding the British Royal Navy (英國皇家海軍) fleet; Admiral Pierre-Charles Villeneuve (維爾納夫), commanding the combined Franco-Spanish fleet.

Event at stake: The Battle of Trafalgar (特拉法加海戰), fought 21 October 1805 off Cape Trafalgar (特拉法加角), Spain, during the Napoleonic Wars (拿破崙戰爭).

Big picture: Napoleon (拿破崙) needed naval control of the English Channel (英倫海峽) to invade Britain; a combined Franco-Spanish fleet sailing from Cadiz to break the British blockade was intercepted by Nelson before it could regroup.

The Battle of Trafalgar (特拉法加海戰), 21 October 1805, painted by William Clarkson Stanfield. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

On the morning of 21 October 1805, Nelson signalled his fleet with the now-famous message: “England expects that every man will do his duty.” His 27 ships of the line faced a combined Franco-Spanish fleet of 33 ships under Villeneuve — a numerical disadvantage Nelson planned to offset not with caution, but with an unconventional and genuinely risky tactic.

Rather than forming the traditional single line parallel to the enemy, Nelson split his fleet into two columns and sailed them perpendicular straight into the enemy’s line, led by his flagship HMS Victory (勝利號). The approach exposed his leading ships to unanswered broadsides for a dangerous stretch of time, but once the columns punched through, they broke the enemy formation into isolated, disorganised segments — ideal conditions for close-quarters fighting, where better-drilled British crews could fire up to three broadsides in the time French and Spanish crews, undertrained after years bottled up in port, could manage one.

The result was decisive: 22 enemy ships captured or destroyed, without the loss of a single British vessel. Nelson himself was shot by a French sniper aboard the Redoutable during the fighting and died that afternoon, reportedly saying “Thank God I have done my duty” upon learning of the victory. The battle ended any real threat of a French invasion of Britain and secured Royal Navy (皇家海軍) dominance of the seas for a century, later formalised in the Treaty of Amiens’ collapse and Napoleon’s pivot toward continental warfare.

「理在氣中，日新又新」：王夫之與明清之際的氣一元論

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
一位明朝遺民學者拒絕出仕清朝，隱居湖南深山四十餘年，將畢生著作藏於箱篋。他主張世界並非由一個先於萬物存在的抽象「理」所主宰，而是「理」就存在於流動不息的「氣」之中，每一天都在更新變化。你認為，這種「日新」的世界觀，會如何影響一個人看待歷史與傳統？
背景速覽

核心人物：王夫之（字而農，號薑齋，晚年隱居湘西石船山，故稱「船山先生」，1619–1692年）。

核心思想：氣一元論——理不能脫離氣而獨立存在，理只在氣之中運行；「日新之化」——世界與歷史並非靜止循環，而是不斷更新演化的動態過程。

時代背景：明清鼎革之際，王夫之目睹國破家亡，與黃宗羲、顧炎武並稱明末清初三大思想家，拒不仕清，隱居著述以明志。

王夫之（船山先生）畫像，明末清初思想家。來源：維基媒體共享資源

王夫之早年投身抗清活動，兵敗後隱居衡陽石船山下，終身不剃髮易服，以「明遺臣」自居，長達四十餘年杜門著述。其著作在生前多未刊行，直至晚清曾國藩兄弟始加以蒐集刊刻，方為後世所知，與黃宗羲、顧炎武並稱明末清初三大思想家。

針對程朱理學「理在氣先」、理是獨立於物質世界之上的抽象本體的觀點，王夫之提出針鋒相對的修正：理只存在於氣之中，並非在氣之先、之外別有一個理。他認為天地萬物皆為氣之聚散變化，理不過是氣運行時所呈現的條理與規律，脫離氣談理只是空中樓閣，這是對程朱理學數百年正統地位的根本性挑戰。

在此基礎上，王夫之進一步提出「日新之化」的歷史觀：氣恆動不息，故天地萬物與人類歷史皆處於持續更新之中，並非簡單的治亂循環或今不如昔的退化過程。這種強調變化與人為努力的史觀，一定程度上肯定了人在歷史進程中的能動作用，也為晚清變法思潮提供了重要的傳統思想資源。

Why “All Swans Are White” Can Never Be Proven — Only Disproven: Karl Popper (卡爾·波普爾) and Falsifiability (可證偽性)

🤔 Predict Before You Read
A philosopher argues that no scientific theory can ever truly be proven, no matter how many confirming observations pile up — but a single contrary piece of evidence can, in principle, disprove it instantly. Before reading on, what do you think this means for how we should tell real science apart from something that only sounds scientific?
Context

Key figures: Karl Popper (卡爾·波普爾, 1902–1994), Austrian-British philosopher; contrasted against the logical positivism (邏輯實證主義) of the Vienna Circle (維也納學圈).

Idea at stake: The demarcation problem (劃界問題) — what distinguishes genuine science from pseudoscience (偽科學) — and whether theories can ever be proven true through accumulated evidence alone.

Big picture: In 1920s Vienna, Popper was struck by a contrast: Einstein’s general relativity (廣義相對論) made a risky, falsifiable prediction confirmed by the 1919 eclipse test, while Marxist and Freudian theories seemed able to explain away almost any evidence.

Karl Popper (卡爾·波普爾), whose falsifiability criterion reshaped the philosophy of science. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Popper’s key move echoed David Hume’s (休謨) problem of induction (歸納問題): no finite number of observations of white swans can ever logically prove the universal claim “all swans are white,” yet a single confirmed sighting of a black swan instantly refutes it. Science, Popper argued, therefore cannot be built on accumulating confirmations alone — proof and disproof are not symmetrical operations.

In The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934/1959), Popper proposed falsifiability (可證偽性) as his criterion of demarcation: a theory only counts as genuinely scientific if it makes risky predictions that could, in principle, be shown false by observation. Physics passed this test easily; Popper controversially argued that Marxism (馬克思主義) and psychoanalysis (精神分析) failed it, since their practitioners could reinterpret almost any outcome as confirming the theory rather than threatening it.

Later philosophers pushed back: the Duhem–Quine thesis (迪昂–奎因論題) noted that auxiliary assumptions can always absorb the blame for a failed prediction instead of the core theory, and Thomas Kuhn (孔恩) argued scientists rarely abandon a paradigm (範式) at the first contrary result. Even so, Popper’s falsifiability test remains the everyday shorthand scientists and journalists still reach for when asking whether a claim is genuinely testable at all.

Check your answer

The “Personality Test” Where Everyone Got the Same Answer: The Barnum Effect (巴納姆效應)

🤔 Predict Before You Read
A psychologist gives students a personality test, then hands back what he claims is each student’s own personalised result. In reality, every single student receives the exact same generic description, assembled from newspaper horoscope columns. Before reading on, how accurately do you think students rated a description that wasn’t even about them?
P. T. Barnum (巴納姆), the showman whose name was lent to the Barnum effect (巴納姆效應) by psychologist Paul Meehl. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In 1948, psychologist Bertram Forer (伯特倫·福勒) gave students a personality assessment, then distributed identical vague, flattering write-ups to everyone — statements like “you have a need for other people to like and admire you” and “at times you have serious doubts as to whether you have made the right decision.” Asked to rate how accurately the description matched them personally on a 0–5 scale, students averaged 4.26, genuinely believing the generic text had been written specifically about them.

Psychologist Paul Meehl (保羅·米爾) later named the phenomenon the Barnum effect (巴納姆效應), after showman P.T. Barnum’s (巴納姆) reputed claim that a good show has “a little something for everybody.” The mechanism combines several biases at once: statements vague enough to fit almost anyone, a mix of flattering and mildly critical claims that feels balanced and honest, and confirmation bias (確認偏誤) that makes people notice the parts that fit while quietly glossing over the parts that don’t.

The effect explains the enduring popularity of horoscopes, tarot readings, and cold reading (冷讀術) performed by psychics — vague statements delivered with confidence feel personally revealing even when they apply to nearly everyone in the room. It has also been used to critique some popular personality tests, which critics argue sometimes package Barnum-style generic feedback as if it were individualised psychological insight.

A Reflex Older Than Lungs? The Strange Mechanics of Hiccups (打嗝)

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Human fetuses (胎兒) begin hiccupping in the womb long before their lungs ever draw a breath of air, sometimes dozens of times a day. Before reading on, why might a body practise a seemingly pointless reflex months before it could possibly serve any purpose?
The diaphragm (橫膈膜) and respiratory system, whose sudden spasm produces a hiccup. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

A hiccup (打嗝) begins with a sudden, involuntary spasm of the diaphragm (橫膈膜) — the dome-shaped muscle beneath the lungs that drives normal breathing — immediately followed by an abrupt closure of the glottis (聲門), the opening between the vocal cords. That sudden closure interrupts the inrush of air, producing the characteristic “hic” sound. The reflex runs through a loop involving the phrenic nerve (膈神經) and vagus nerve (迷走神經), coordinated by a “hiccup centre” in the brainstem (腦幹).

Common triggers include eating too quickly, carbonated drinks, alcohol, and sudden temperature changes, all of which can irritate the relevant nerves or stretch the stomach enough to set off the reflex. Most bouts resolve within minutes on their own; the traditional folk remedies — holding your breath, drinking water upside down, being startled — largely work by raising blood carbon dioxide levels or stimulating the vagus nerve, both of which help interrupt and reset the reflex loop.

One evolutionary theory, proposed by biologist Daniel Howes in 2012, suggests hiccups may be a vestige of the neural circuitry amphibian tadpoles (蝌蚪) use to push water past their gills while keeping their lungs sealed shut — a hypothesis still debated among scientists. What’s less debated is that hiccups appear remarkably early in human development, showing up in fetuses well before birth, hinting the reflex may be far more ancient than the lungs it now interrupts.

Standing All Day Isn’t the Fix Either: What the Standing Desk (企立式辦公桌) Evidence Actually Shows

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Prolonged sitting has been linked to higher risk of heart disease and early death, even among people who exercise regularly — a finding that helped fuel a wave of office standing desks. Before reading on, do you think simply standing instead of sitting at your desk meaningfully lowers that health risk?
A standing desk (企立式辦公桌) setup, compared against a seated posture. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

An influential 2015 meta-analysis in the Annals of Internal Medicine, led by Aviroop Biswas, found that prolonged sitting time was associated with significantly higher risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, and all-cause mortality — and notably, the association held even among people who met standard weekly exercise guidelines, suggesting sitting itself carries some risk independent of simply “not exercising enough.”

That finding fuelled the popularity of standing desks, but randomised trials testing them directly, including the “Stand Up Victoria” workplace trial, found more modest results: standing desks reliably reduce total daily sitting time, but evidence for meaningful weight loss or major improvements in blood sugar and cholesterol markers has been weak and inconsistent. Standing itself burns only marginally more calories than sitting — roughly an extra 0.7 calories per minute — nowhere near enough to offset a sedentary diet or substitute for real exercise.

Standing all day brings its own problems too, including a higher risk of varicose veins (靜脈曲張), lower back pain, and fatigue from prolonged static posture. Physiologist James Levine’s (詹姆斯·萊文) concept of NEAT (非運動性熱效應, non-exercise activity thermogenesis) points to a better target than either sitting or standing alone: frequent movement breaks, roughly every 30 minutes, appear to matter more for metabolic health than which position you hold your body in for hours at a stretch.

Why Lifting Heavy Right Before You Jump Can Make You Jump Higher: Post-Activation Potentiation (激活後增強效應)

🤔 Predict Before You Read
A sprinter performs a single, near-maximal heavy squat, rests for several minutes, then attempts an explosive jump — and jumps noticeably higher than if they had warmed up without the heavy lift at all. Before reading on, how do you think briefly fatiguing your muscles with a heavy weight could end up making your next explosive movement better, not worse?
Heavy strength training paired with an explosive movement is the basis of complex training (複合式訓練). Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Post-activation potentiation, or PAP (激活後增強效應), describes a real, measurable phenomenon in which a heavy, near-maximal muscular contraction temporarily enhances the force and speed of a subsequent explosive movement performed by the same muscle group, provided the two are separated by an appropriate rest interval — commonly used by pairing a heavy back squat with a vertical jump or short sprint in what is called “complex training” (複合式訓練).

The mechanism runs through two channels: at the muscle level, the heavy contraction phosphorylates myosin regulatory light chains (肌凝蛋白調節輕鏈), making the muscle fibres more sensitive to calcium and improving cross-bridge cycling efficiency; at the nervous-system level, the heavy lift increases motor unit recruitment (運動單位徵召) and neural drive, priming the same muscles to fire more forcefully on the next explosive effort.

The tricky part is timing: too little rest after the heavy lift and residual fatigue outweighs any potentiation benefit; too much rest and the effect fades away entirely. Research reviews, including work by Sale and by Docherty and Hodgson, generally find the sweet spot falls somewhere between four and twelve minutes, though the ideal window varies considerably by individual, with stronger, more trained athletes typically showing a larger and more reliable PAP response than novices.

The Nobel-Winning Insight Hiding in “Don’t Put All Your Eggs in One Basket”: Harry Markowitz (哈里·馬科維茨) and Modern Portfolio Theory (現代投資組合理論)

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Everyone knows the old advice not to put all your eggs in one basket. Before reading on, what do you think a mathematically rigorous version of that advice would need to specify, beyond just “own more than one thing”?
The efficient frontier (效率前緣), Harry Markowitz’s chart of optimal risk-return combinations. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In 1952, a 25-year-old graduate student named Harry Markowitz (哈里·馬科維茨) published a nine-page paper in the Journal of Finance titled “Portfolio Selection” that quietly transformed how professional investors think about risk. His key insight went beyond simply owning many assets: what actually reduces a portfolio’s overall risk is combining assets whose prices don’t move in lockstep with each other — low or negative correlation (相關性) matters more than sheer number of holdings.

Markowitz formalised this into mean-variance optimisation (均值-方差最適化): for any target level of expected return, there exists a specific combination of assets that minimises risk, and plotting these optimal combinations traces out what is called the efficient frontier (效率前緣) — the boundary of portfolios offering the best possible return for a given level of risk, with anything below the frontier considered needlessly inefficient.

The framework earned Markowitz the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (諾貝爾經濟學獎), shared with Merton Miller and William Sharpe, and became the theoretical foundation for the Capital Asset Pricing Model (資本資產定價模型) and much of modern index investing. Its major blind spot emerged starkly in the 2008 financial crisis: the model assumes stable correlations, but in a systemic crash, assets that appeared uncorrelated for decades can suddenly move together — diversification’s protection tends to weaken exactly when investors need it most.

⚖️ Political Economics — Theory in the Wild

When the Textbook Prescription Didn’t Cure the Patient: The Washington Consensus (華盛頓共識)

The International Monetary Fund (國際貨幣基金組織), one of the institutions behind the Washington Consensus (華盛頓共識) prescriptions. Credit: Wikimedia Commons
🌍 Event
In the 1980s, Latin American economies buckled under a severe debt crisis (債務危機); in exchange for emergency loans, the International Monetary Fund (國際貨幣基金組織) and World Bank (世界銀行) required governments to adopt a standardised package of reforms as loan conditionality. Argentina followed the prescription closely through the 1990s — fixed currency board, mass privatisation, trade liberalisation — yet collapsed into a catastrophic economic crisis in 2001.
📖 Theory
Economist John Williamson (約翰·威廉森) coined the term “Washington Consensus” in 1989 to describe roughly ten policy prescriptions favoured by Washington-based institutions: fiscal discipline, trade and financial liberalisation, privatisation of state enterprises, deregulation, and secure property rights — reflecting the belief that market-oriented reform, properly sequenced, would restore growth and stability to struggling economies.
⚠️ Anomaly
Results across the region were decidedly mixed: Argentina’s 2001 collapse came despite textbook compliance, while China (中國) achieved sustained rapid growth through the same decades using a heterodox, gradualist approach that violated many Consensus tenets — retaining capital controls, pursuing gradual rather than rapid privatisation, and relying heavily on state-directed investment.
🧪 Challenger
Economist Joseph Stiglitz (約瑟夫·斯蒂格利茨) argued in his “post-Washington Consensus” critique that reform sequencing and institution-building must precede market liberalisation, not follow it; Dani Rodrik (達尼·羅德里克) similarly contends that context-specific, gradualist reforms outperform one-size-fits-all prescriptions — though how much of the region’s divergent outcomes reflects institutions, sequencing, or simply luck remains genuinely disputed.
💡 A reform package that works on a spreadsheet can still fail a country — sequencing and institutions turned out to matter as much as the policies themselves.
📖 Williamson, J. (1990). “What Washington Means by Policy Reform,” Institute for International Economics. Stiglitz, J. (2002). Globalization and Its Discontents. Rodrik, D. (2006). Journal of Economic Literature, 44(4).

🔗 Connection of the Day

Cards 5 & 6 — Vagueness Feels Like Insight: Popper’s falsifiability test and the Barnum effect are two sides of the same coin. A horoscope-style personality reading rated “highly accurate” by students survives scrutiny precisely because it is vague enough to never be wrong — the exact trait Popper flagged as disqualifying a claim from counting as genuine science.

So what: The next time a claim — a personality test, a prediction, a theory — feels impressively accurate, ask what specific outcome would have proven it wrong. If nothing could have, that feeling of accuracy is likely an illusion built from vagueness, not real insight.
