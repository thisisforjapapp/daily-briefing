The Cracked Ice World With an Ocean Hiding Beneath

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In September 2022, NASA’s Juno spacecraft swooped within 358 kilometres of one of Jupiter’s four largest moons, capturing its closest images in over two decades. Before reading on, what do you think the reddish-brown cracks streaking across this moon’s icy shell in the photos actually are, and why do scientists think they matter?
Europa’s (歐羅巴) fractured ice shell, imaged by NASA’s Juno (朱諾號) spacecraft during its September 2022 flyby at just 358 km altitude. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

On 29 September 2022, NASA’s (美國太空總署) Juno (朱諾號) spacecraft swept past Europa (歐羅巴), one of Jupiter’s (木星) four large Galilean moons (伽利略衛星), at an altitude of just 358 kilometres, its closest human-made encounter with the moon in over twenty years. The images beamed back showed a startling landscape: a smooth, icy shell riddled with long, reddish-brown cracks called lineae (線紋), some stretching thousands of kilometres, crossing and overlapping like scars on a cracked eggshell. Unlike the heavily cratered moons nearby, Europa’s surface looked almost eerily young and unbroken by impacts.

Scientists had suspected since NASA’s Galileo (伽利略號) mission in the 1990s that these fractures aren’t ordinary surface features but evidence of something extraordinary underneath: a global liquid-water ocean (海洋), estimated to hold roughly twice the water of all Earth’s oceans combined, sealed beneath an ice shell 15 to 25 kilometres thick. The cracks form as Jupiter’s immense gravity flexes Europa’s interior with every orbit, a process called tidal heating (潮汐加熱), generating enough warmth to keep that ocean liquid despite Europa sitting over 600 million kilometres from the Sun (太陽), far beyond where sunlight alone could sustain liquid water.

The reddish colour staining the cracks may come from salts or sulfur compounds welling up from the ocean below, chemistry that intrigues astrobiologists because Earth’s own deep-sea hydrothermal vents host life without sunlight. NASA’s Europa Clipper (歐羅巴快船), launched in October 2024, is now travelling to conduct dozens of close flybys starting in 2030, hunting for plumes of water vapour erupting through the ice and mapping the shell’s thickness, the most detailed attempt yet to answer whether this cracked, icy moon could harbour the conditions for life beyond Earth.

一個書生如何以新法對抗整個官僚體制

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
北宋熙寧二年（1069年），一位以文章聞名、素以生活儉樸著稱的翰林學士，突然獲宋神宗破格拔擢主持變法，推行一連串觸動地主、官僚、甚至皇室利益的新政。你認為，這樣一場由書生主導、觸犯既得利益的改革，最終命運會是甚麼？
背景速覽

核心人物：王安石（1021–1086，江西臨川人，翰林學士、宰相）；宋神宗；司馬光（反對派領袖）。

事件：熙寧變法（1069–1085），推行青苗法、免役法、市易法、保甲法等新政。

時代背景：北宋中葉冗官冗兵冗費，國庫空虛，西北邊患未除，士大夫階層對變法路線爭論激烈。

王安石畫像，北宋熙寧變法的主導者，推行青苗、免役、市易等新法。來源：維基媒體共享資源

宋神宗熙寧二年（1069年），北宋立國已逾百年，冗官、冗兵、冗費三大積弊日益嚴重，國庫年年虧空，西北又長期受西夏侵擾，朝廷內外憂心忡忡。時年四十八歲的王安石，早年在地方任官政績卓著，以《上仁宗皇帝言事書》聞名於世，主張大刀闊斧改革，獲神宗賞識，破格擢升為參知政事，隨即設立「制置三司條例司」，開始推行一連串史稱「熙寧變法」的新政，鋒芒直指北宋積弱不振的根本問題。

王安石新法涵蓋財政、軍事、科舉多個層面：青苗法由官府在青黃不接時向農民放貸，取代民間高利貸盤剝；免役法讓百姓繳錢代役，官府另僱人應役；市易法由官府平抑物價、調節市場；保甲法則將農戶編組訓練，寓兵於農。這些新法意在增加國庫收入、減輕百姓負擔，然而執行過程中地方官員為求政績強行攤派，青苗法貸款利息實際加重了部分農民負擔，引發司馬光、蘇軾等士大夫強烈反對，朝廷因而分裂為新舊兩黨，爭論持續數十年。

司馬光屢次上書痛陳新法擾民，與王安石在御前展開激烈辯論，兩人本為舊識，終因政見不合而絕交。神宗駕崩後，年幼的哲宗繼位，太皇太后高氏臨朝，起用司馬光為相，新法幾乎盡數廢除，史稱「元祐更化」；及後哲宗親政又恢復新法，政局隨新舊兩黨輪替反覆拉鋸，史稱「新舊黨爭」，最終消耗了北宋朝廷大量精力，未能根治積弱局面，反而加深了黨爭傷害，為日後靖康之難埋下伏筆。

The One-Armed Admiral Who Broke Napoleon’s Invasion Plan Without Losing a Ship

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In October 1805, a British fleet commanded by an admiral who had already lost an eye and an arm in earlier battles sailed to intercept a combined French and Spanish fleet off Spain’s Atlantic coast. Before reading on, what unconventional tactic do you think he used against a larger enemy force, and why might it have cost him his life even in victory?
Context

Key figures: Vice-Admiral Horatio Nelson (納爾遜, 1758–1805), commander of the British (英國) fleet; Admiral Pierre-Charles Villeneuve (維爾納夫), commander of the combined French-Spanish fleet.

Event at stake: The Battle of Trafalgar (特拉法加海戰), 21 October 1805, off Cape Trafalgar, Spain (西班牙).

Big picture: Napoleon (拿破崙) needed naval control of the English Channel (英倫海峽) to invade Britain; this battle decided whether that invasion could ever happen.

The Battle of Trafalgar (特拉法加海戰), 21 October 1805 — Nelson’s (納爾遜) fleet broke the combined French-Spanish line in two, ending Napoleon’s hopes of invading Britain by sea. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

By 1805, Napoleon (拿破崙) had assembled an army of over 150,000 men at Boulogne (布倫), poised to cross the English Channel (英倫海峽) and invade Britain (英國), but the plan depended entirely on French and Spanish (西班牙) fleets first winning control of the Channel from the Royal Navy (皇家海軍). When the combined fleet under Admiral Pierre-Charles Villeneuve (維爾納夫) sailed from Cádiz in October 1805, Vice-Admiral Horatio Nelson (納爾遜), already blind in one eye and missing an arm from earlier campaigns, moved to intercept it off Cape Trafalgar (特拉法加角) on Spain’s Atlantic coast.

Naval convention dictated fleets fight in a single parallel line, exchanging broadsides at a distance. Nelson instead split his 27 ships into two columns and drove them perpendicular straight into the 33-ship enemy line, a risky manoeuvre exposing his lead ships to concentrated fire during the approach but designed to break the enemy formation into chaotic, close-range melees where superior British gunnery crews, who could fire roughly three broadsides for every two of their opponents, would dominate. The tactic worked decisively: 22 French and Spanish ships were captured or destroyed, while Britain lost none.

During the height of the battle, a French sniper aboard the Redoutable shot Nelson through the shoulder and spine as he walked his flagship’s deck in full uniform; he died roughly three hours later, reportedly asking to hear that victory was confirmed before his final words: “Thank God, I have done my duty.” The victory permanently destroyed Napoleon’s invasion fleet and any realistic prospect of a cross-Channel invasion, securing British naval supremacy for the following century and turning Nelson into Britain’s most enduring military legend, commemorated today by Nelson’s Column (納爾遜紀念柱) in London’s Trafalgar Square.

一句「白馬非馬」如何撼動整個名實世界

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
戰國時期，一位思想家在趙國邊境向守關官員宣稱「白馬非馬」，聲稱這匹白馬不算是一匹馬，因而不受「馬不得出關」的禁令限制。你認為，這聽起來荒謬的論辯，背後究竟藏著甚麼樣的邏輯？
背景速覽

核心人物：公孫龍（約前320–前250年，趙國人，名家代表人物）。

核心思想：「白馬非馬」論、「離堅白」論，探討「名」（概念）與「實」（實物）之間的關係。

時代背景：戰國諸子百家爭鳴，名家專注於邏輯與語言分析，與儒墨道法諸家並立，卻因議題抽象而較少被後世重視。

公孫龍畫像，戰國名家代表人物，以「白馬非馬」論聞名於世。來源：維基媒體共享資源

公孫龍，趙國人，戰國中後期思想家，屬於「名家」一派，專門研究「名」（概念、名稱）與「實」（具體事物）之間的邏輯關係，這在諸子百家中是相對冷門卻極具思辨深度的領域。相傳公孫龍曾騎白馬欲出函谷關，關吏依禁令阻攔，理由是「馬不得出關」，公孫龍卻辯稱「白馬非馬」，因而不受此禁令約束，最終竟真的說服關吏放行，此事後世稱為「白馬非馬」之辯，成為中國邏輯史上最著名的詭辯案例之一。

公孫龍的論證邏輯在於：「馬」這個概念指涉的是形態，「白」指涉的是顏色，「白馬」則同時包含形態與顏色兩重規定性，其外延範圍比單純的「馬」更為狹窄，因此「白馬」與「馬」在概念上並非完全等同的「名」，故謂「白馬非馬」。這一論證雖然表面近乎文字遊戲，實質上觸及了「共相」與「殊相」的哲學問題，即抽象概念與具體事物之間是否存在必然對應關係，與古希臘柏拉圖的理型論有異曲同工之處，卻在中國思想史上鮮少獲得同等重視。

公孫龍另有「離堅白」論，主張堅硬與白色雖同屬一塊石頭的屬性，卻是透過觸覺與視覺兩種不同感官分別認知，二者在概念上應予「離析」，不可混為一談。這類論辯在儒家荀子眼中被批評為「蔽於辭而不知實」，墨家後學亦曾撰文反駁名家的詭辯傾向，名家學派因而在漢代以後逐漸式微，其著作大多散佚，僅存《公孫龍子》殘篇。然而近代學者重新審視名家的邏輯思辨，肯定其在中國古代邏輯學發展史上的獨特地位。

🌿 Philosophy — Western

The Philosopher Who Had a Breakdown at Twenty and Rewrote What Freedom Means

🤔 Predict Before You Read
As a child, this philosopher was taught Greek at age three and had read most of Plato in the original by eight, the product of an intense experimental education designed by his father. Before reading on, what do you think happened to him at twenty that changed his entire philosophy, and what famous idea about individual freedom did he go on to develop?
Context

Key figure: John Stuart Mill (穆勒, 1806–1873), English (英國) philosopher and economist (經濟學家).

Idea at stake: The “harm principle” (傷害原則) from his 1859 essay On Liberty (《論自由》): individual liberty should only be restricted to prevent harm to others.

Big picture: A response to growing anxiety about the “tyranny of the majority” (多數人的暴政) in an increasingly democratic (民主) 19th-century Britain.

John Stuart Mill (穆勒), whose 1859 essay On Liberty (《論自由》) argued individual freedom should only be restricted to prevent harm to others. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

John Stuart Mill (穆勒) was subjected to an extraordinary childhood experiment by his father, the philosopher James Mill (詹姆士·穆勒), who taught him Greek (希臘文) at age three, Latin (拉丁文) at eight, and had him reading Plato (柏拉圖) and studying logic and political economy before most children finished primary school, all designed to produce a rigorously rational utilitarian (功利主義者) thinker. At twenty, Mill suffered a severe mental breakdown, later describing in his Autobiography a period of complete emotional numbness, questioning whether achieving all his father’s utilitarian goals would actually make him happy, a crisis that pushed him toward valuing emotion, poetry, and individuality alongside pure reason.

That crisis reshaped his mature philosophy. In his 1859 essay On Liberty (《論自由》), co-developed with his intellectual partner Harriet Taylor Mill (哈莉特·泰勒·穆勒), Mill argued for what became known as the “harm principle” (傷害原則): the only justification for restricting an individual’s actions against their will is to prevent harm to others, not to protect them from themselves or to enforce majority moral preferences. He worried openly about a “tyranny of the majority” (多數人的暴政) emerging even in democracies, where social conformity could crush unconventional opinions and lifestyles just as effectively as any government censorship.

Mill extended this reasoning into utilitarianism (功利主義) itself, breaking from his father’s and Jeremy Bentham’s (邊沁) view that all pleasures count equally, famously arguing it is “better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied,” insisting some pleasures, intellectual and moral ones, are qualitatively higher than mere physical satisfaction. Critics have long debated whether this move quietly abandons utilitarianism’s simplicity for something closer to virtue ethics, but On Liberty remains a foundational text for modern free-speech law and civil liberties, still cited in court rulings and political debate over where individual freedom should end and collective interest begin.

The Self-Experiment That Mapped Exactly How Fast You Forget

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In the 1880s, a German psychologist spent years memorising thousands of meaningless three-letter nonsense syllables, testing himself repeatedly on how much he’d forgotten after minutes, hours, and days. Before reading on, what do you think his results revealed about the shape of forgetting, and why might that same data still shape how students study today?
Hermann Ebbinghaus (艾賓浩斯), whose 1885 self-experiments on memorising nonsense syllables produced the first quantitative model of forgetting. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In the 1880s, German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus (艾賓浩斯) became his own experimental subject, memorising over 2,300 meaningless three-letter nonsense syllables, combinations like “WUX” or “CAZ” chosen specifically to have no prior associations, then testing how many repetitions he needed to relearn each list after different time delays. Published in 1885, his results produced what’s now called the “forgetting curve” (遺忘曲線): memory retention drops steeply within the first hour after learning, then the rate of loss gradually slows, so information surviving the first day tends to stick around considerably longer than that lost in the first hour.

Ebbinghaus also discovered something with direct practical value: revisiting material at spaced intervals, rather than cramming it all at once, dramatically improved long-term retention compared to the same total study time compressed together, an effect now called the “spacing effect” (間隔效應). Reviewing a fact right before you’d naturally forget it, rather than reviewing something already firmly memorised, appears to force the brain to actively reconstruct the memory trace, strengthening it far more than passive re-exposure to material still fresh in mind.

A large 2006 meta-analysis by Nicholas Cepeda (塞佩達) and colleagues, reviewing over 250 studies spanning more than a century, confirmed the spacing effect holds robustly across ages, subjects, and memory types, forming the scientific basis behind modern spaced-repetition (間隔重複) apps like Anki. The main limitation is that Ebbinghaus’s original curve came from meaningless syllables under artificial lab conditions; real-world, meaningful information tied to existing knowledge tends to fade more slowly, and emotional or highly relevant memories can resist the curve almost entirely.

🫀 Physiology

The Sharp Pain Under Your Ribs That Stops Runners Mid-Stride

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Almost everyone who has run, swum, or exercised hard has felt it: a sudden, sharp stabbing pain just under one side of the ribcage that forces you to slow down or stop entirely. Before reading on, what do you think is physically happening inside your body to cause this, and why does it almost always disappear once you stop moving?
The diaphragm (橫膈膜), the dome-shaped muscle beneath the lungs, is closely linked to the membrane thought to cause exercise-related side stitches. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

The sudden, sharp pain runners and swimmers feel just below the ribcage during exercise, medically termed exercise-related transient abdominal pain (運動相關短暫性腹痛), or ETAP, and commonly called a “side stitch,” affects an estimated one in five runners during any given race. Despite how common it is, scientists only settled on a leading explanation relatively recently: for decades the dominant theory blamed a simple lack of blood flow or oxygen to the diaphragm (橫膈膜), the dome-shaped muscle beneath the lungs that drives breathing, treating it essentially as a cramp.

More recent research points instead to irritation of the parietal peritoneum (壁腹膜), a thin membrane lining the abdominal cavity and connecting to the diaphragm via ligaments. During running, repetitive jarring motion and a full stomach or bladder can stretch and pull on this membrane, especially where it attaches near the liver on the right side, explaining why stitches occur far more often on the right than the left, and why drinking a large, sugary sports drink right before exercise noticeably raises the risk.

This explains the common remedies that actually work: slowing down reduces the jarring motion irritating the membrane, while exhaling forcefully when the foot on the opposite side of the pain strikes the ground can ease the mechanical tug on the diaphragm’s attachments. Avoiding large meals or sugary drinks in the hour before intense exercise, and strengthening core and postural muscles through regular training, both appear to reduce how often stitches occur, though researchers still don’t fully understand why some people are far more prone to them than others.

Why a Spoonful of Vinegar Won’t Melt Your Fat Away

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Apple cider vinegar has been marketed for over a decade as a near-miracle weight-loss aid, with viral claims that a tablespoon before meals can suppress appetite and burn fat. Before reading on, what do you think the actual clinical evidence shows about how much weight, if any, this can realistically help someone lose?
Vinegar (醋) — apple cider vinegar’s active ingredient, acetic acid, has modest, short-lived metabolic effects, not the dramatic fat-burning claimed by viral marketing. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Apple cider vinegar (蘋果醋) contains acetic acid (乙酸), the same compound giving all vinegar its sour taste, and it’s this ingredient that most weight-loss claims hinge on. Early small studies, including a widely cited 2009 Japanese trial, found participants drinking vinegar daily lost modestly more weight than a placebo group over 12 weeks, roughly 1 to 2 kilograms, and some research shows acetic acid can slightly slow stomach emptying and blunt post-meal blood sugar (血糖) spikes, giving the claims a kernel of real physiology rather than pure fabrication.

However, a 2024 systematic review pooling multiple randomised trials found the weight-loss effect, while statistically present in some studies, is small, inconsistent across different populations, and often confounded by participants simultaneously reducing calorie intake or changing other habits alongside the vinegar regimen, common in studies that aren’t tightly controlled. Some trials, including a heavily publicised 2024 study claiming dramatic results in teenagers, have faced serious scrutiny over data irregularities and were flagged by independent researchers, undermining confidence in the most eye-catching headline numbers circulating online.

Undiluted vinegar’s acidity also poses real risks: case reports document esophageal (食道) burns and tooth enamel (牙齒琺瑯質) erosion from drinking it directly or in high concentrations, meaning any potential benefit must be weighed against genuine harm from misuse. The realistic takeaway is that apple cider vinegar, diluted and taken occasionally, is not dangerous for most healthy adults and may offer a marginal effect on blood sugar, but it is not a substitute for the calorie deficit, sleep, and exercise that actually drive meaningful, sustained weight loss.

Does Rolling Around on a Foam Cylinder Actually Do Anything?

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Foam rollers, dense cylinders of compressed foam that gym-goers press their body weight against and roll over sore muscles, have become nearly as common in gyms as dumbbells. Before reading on, what do you think the research actually shows this practice does to muscle tissue, and does it genuinely speed up recovery?
Sports recovery tools like foam rollers are widely used alongside massage in athletic training rooms. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Foam rolling, technically called self-myofascial release (自我肌筋膜放鬆), involves applying sustained pressure with a firm foam cylinder to muscle and the surrounding fascia (筋膜), the connective tissue sheath wrapping muscle groups, with practitioners typically rolling slowly over sore areas for 30 to 60 seconds. The original theoretical claim was dramatic: that foam rolling could physically break up “adhesions” (沾黏) or “knots” in the fascia, restoring proper tissue sliding and range of motion, a mechanical explanation that turned out to be largely unsupported once researchers actually measured what the pressure applied by a foam roller could physically achieve.

The pressure a person can realistically generate through body weight on a foam roller is far too low to meaningfully deform dense fascial tissue, which studies show requires forces well beyond what casual rolling produces. Instead, current research points to neurological and perceptual mechanisms: foam rolling appears to temporarily reduce the nervous system’s pain-sensitivity threshold in the rolled area and may modestly improve short-term range of motion, likely through changed tolerance to stretch and pressure sensation rather than any physical restructuring of the tissue itself.

A 2020 meta-analysis by Wiewelhove (維維霍夫) and colleagues, reviewing multiple recovery-focused trials, found foam rolling after exercise produces small but real reductions in delayed-onset muscle soreness (延遲性肌肉痠痛) and modest improvements in perceived recovery, comparable in magnitude to light massage, without the strength or power deficits associated with static stretching. It’s a low-risk, low-cost addition to a recovery routine that likely helps a little through the nervous system rather than the dramatic tissue-fixing story originally sold to justify it.

📈 Investing & Economic Thinking

The Company That Invented the Digital Camera and Then Killed It to Protect Film

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In 1975, an engineer at a photography company built the world’s first working digital camera, a toaster-sized prototype that took 23 seconds to capture a single black-and-white image. Before reading on, what do you think the company’s leadership decided to do with this invention, and why might that decision explain the company’s eventual bankruptcy?
Eastman Kodak (伊士曼柯達) — the company invented the digital camera in 1975 but shelved it for decades to protect its dominant film business. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In 1975, Eastman Kodak (伊士曼柯達) engineer Steven Sasson (薩森) built the world’s first working digital camera, a bulky prototype the size of a toaster that recorded black-and-white images onto a cassette tape at a resolution of 0.01 megapixels, taking 23 seconds to capture and save a single photo. Sasson later recalled demonstrating it to Kodak executives, who found the technology fascinating but asked him not to talk about it outside the company, reportedly telling him: “that’s cute, but don’t tell anyone about it,” a reaction that set the tone for how Kodak would treat digital photography for the next two decades.

Kodak’s hesitation wasn’t irrational stupidity; it was a rational response to a genuine dilemma. The company earned enormous profits from film, chemicals, and photo processing, a business model digital photography would directly cannibalise, and internal analysts reasonably worried that promoting a filmless future would accelerate the collapse of Kodak’s most profitable division long before digital cameras could replace that revenue. Kodak actually held key digital patents and even released digital cameras starting in the 1990s, but consistently prioritised protecting its film business over aggressively pursuing the digital transition, moving cautiously while competitors like Canon (佳能) and Sony (索尼) moved fast.

By the early 2000s, digital cameras and eventually camera phones had made consumer film photography largely obsolete, and Kodak’s revenue collapsed alongside it; the company filed for bankruptcy protection in January 2012, a stunning fall for a business that once commanded roughly 90% of the American film market. Business scholars now cite Kodak as the textbook case of the “innovator’s dilemma” (創新者的兩難), where a company’s existing profitable business model creates powerful internal incentives to delay or suppress a disruptive technology it may have invented itself, protecting near-term earnings at the cost of long-term survival.

⚖️ Political Economics — Theory in the Wild

The Happiness Puzzle That’s Divided Economists for 50 Years

World GDP per capita (人均GDP) has risen dramatically over the past two centuries — but does reported happiness rise with it? Credit: Wikimedia Commons
🌍 Event
In 1974, economist Richard Easterlin (伊斯特林) compared happiness surveys across countries and over time within the United States (美國), spanning decades of dramatic post-war growth (經濟增長), and found something the standard growth-equals-progress story didn’t predict.
📖 Theory
Standard economics assumes rising income (收入) should straightforwardly raise wellbeing (福祉): more income buys more goods, security, and choices, so national happiness should track GDP per capita (人均GDP) growth over time.
⚠️ Anomaly
Easterlin found richer individuals within a country reported more happiness than poorer ones, yet as whole nations grew wealthier over decades, average happiness barely moved; US real income roughly tripled 1946–1970 while life satisfaction stayed flat.
🧪 Challenger
Economists Betsey Stevenson (史蒂文森) and Justin Wolfers (沃爾弗斯) challenged this in 2008, using richer data to argue happiness does rise with GDP, just logarithmically, so equal percentage gains matter more to poor countries than rich ones. The debate remains open.
💡 Getting richer as a nation doesn’t reliably make people happier — but getting richer than your neighbour still might.
📖 Easterlin, R.A. (1974), “Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot?” Stevenson, B. & Wolfers, J. (2008), Brookings Papers on Economic Activity.

🔗 Connection of the Day

Cards 4 & 11 — When the Label Isn’t the Thing: Gongsun Long’s “a white horse is not a horse” (公孫龍「白馬非馬」) argument insists that a name and the reality it points to are never perfectly identical, since “horse” and “white horse” carry different conceptual boundaries. The Easterlin Paradox makes the same point in economics: GDP per capita is a name for prosperity, but it isn’t prosperity itself, and for decades it rose while the thing it was supposed to represent, human happiness, stayed flat. Both cards warn against mistaking a convenient label or metric for the messier reality underneath it.

So what: Whenever you’re handed a single number, a name, or a metric, ask what got left out of the boundary that number drew, before treating it as the whole truth.
