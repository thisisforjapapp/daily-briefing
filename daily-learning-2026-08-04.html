The Ice Moon That Sprayed Its Ocean Into Space

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In 2005, NASA’s Cassini spacecraft flew low over a small, icy moon of Saturn barely 500 kilometres across, previously dismissed as too tiny and cold to be interesting. Before reading on, what do you think its cameras caught erupting from cracks near the moon’s south pole, and why did it turn this overlooked moon into one of the solar system’s most exciting places to search for life?
Enceladus (土衛二), Saturn’s (土星) small icy moon — Cassini’s (卡西尼號) 2005 flybys found water-ice plumes erupting from cracks at its south pole. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

On 14 July 2005, NASA’s (美國太空總署) Cassini (卡西尼號) spacecraft swept low over Enceladus (土衛二), a small icy moon of Saturn (土星) barely 500 kilometres across, previously dismissed as too tiny and cold to be geologically interesting. Its cameras revealed four parallel fractures near the south pole nicknamed “tiger stripes” (虎紋), unusually warm and startlingly young by crater-counting standards. Later that year, images caught something extraordinary erupting from the cracks: towering plumes (羽流) of icy spray shooting hundreds of kilometres into space, visible even backlit against the blackness beyond Saturn’s rings (土星環).

Follow-up flybys, including a 2008 pass where Cassini (卡西尼號) deliberately flew straight through the plume to “taste” it with onboard instruments, confirmed the spray was overwhelmingly water vapour laced with tiny ice grains, simple organic molecules, and microscopic salt-rich particles. That salt content mattered enormously: it meant the water had been in contact with rock, evidence the jets were venting directly from a liquid ocean sloshing beneath Enceladus’s icy shell, not simply sublimating surface frost. More than 100 individual jets combine to form each plume, powered by tidal heating (潮汐加熱) as Saturn’s gravity continuously flexes the moon’s interior.

The discovery transformed Enceladus from an obscure dot into one of the solar system’s (太陽系) most promising places to search for life, since its ocean sits atop a rocky core where hydrothermal vents (熱液噴口), similar to those hosting life on Earth’s ocean floor, appear to be active. NASA (美國太空總署) has since proposed dedicated Enceladus orbiter and lander missions to fly through the plume again and directly test its chemistry for signs of biology, turning a 2005 flyby image into one of astrobiology’s most tantalising open questions.

一代文臣如何被政敵構陷至客死異鄉

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
平安時代一位以學問聞名、官至右大臣的文人，卻在901年被政敵誣告謀反，貶至九州；你認為，這場政治鬥爭背後真正的原因是甚麼？
背景速覽

核心人物：菅原道真（845–903，平安時代學者、右大臣）；藤原時平（左大臣，政敵）；醍醐天皇。

事件：昌泰之變（901年），道真被誣告陰謀廢立天皇，貶為太宰權帥，流放九州太宰府。

時代背景：平安時代中期藤原氏外戚專權日盛，非藤原出身的文人官員位居高位，觸動了藤原氏既得利益集團。

菅原道真死後被奉祀為「天神」的畫像，今日仍是日本學問之神信仰的核心。來源：維基媒體共享資源

菅原道真出身於代代以學問侍奉朝廷的菅原家族，自幼博覽經史，擅長漢詩文，年輕時已以文章博士身分聞名朝野。醍醐天皇即位後，破格擢升道真為右大臣，與出身藤原氏的左大臣藤原時平同列宰相之位，這在當時門第森嚴、藤原氏長期壟斷外戚要職的朝廷中極為罕見，也因此埋下了日後政爭的種子。道真主張限制遣唐使、重視內政整頓，其政治主張與藤原時平代表的貴族既得利益集團屢生齟齬。

昌泰四年（901年），藤原時平向醍醐天皇密奏，指控道真陰謀廢黜天皇，改立齊世親王，圖謀不軌。史書並無確鑿證據支持這項指控，後世史家普遍認為這是藤原時平為排除異己、鞏固藤原氏權力而蓄意構陷的政治誣告。醍醐天皇未加深究，即下詔將道真貶為太宰權帥，遠謫九州太宰府，形同流放，其子女亦多遭連坐貶謫，家族仕途自此一蹶不振。

道真在太宰府度過孤苦的最後兩年，生活拮据，僅能以簡陋官舍棲身，寫下大量思鄉悲憤的漢詩，於延喜三年（903年）鬱鬱而終。他死後，京城接連發生藤原時平早逝、皇太子相繼夭折、清涼殿遭雷擊起火等異象，朝廷驚懼，認為是道真怨靈作祟，遂追復其官位，並建北野天滿宮奉祀，尊為「天神」，使他由含冤而死的政治犧牲者，轉化為日本至今香火鼎盛的學問之神。

How a Starving, Outnumbered Army Destroyed French Chivalry in Three Hours

🤔 Predict Before You Read
On 25 October 1415, an exhausted English army of roughly 6,000 men, many suffering from dysentery, faced a French force nearly four times its size on a muddy field in northern France. Before reading on, what do you think let the smaller army win so decisively, and why did French losses vastly outnumber English ones?
Context

Key figures: King Henry V (亨利五世) of England (英格蘭); the French (法國) nobility and knights under Constable Charles d’Albret.

Event at stake: The Battle of Agincourt (阿金庫爾戰役), 25 October 1415, during the Hundred Years’ War (英法百年戰爭).

Big picture: Henry’s invading army, weakened by dysentery and retreating toward Calais, was trapped by a much larger French force determined to end the campaign decisively.

The Battle of Agincourt (阿金庫爾戰役), 25 October 1415 — mud and terrain turned French numerical superiority into a fatal trap. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

By October 1415, King Henry V’s (亨利五世) invasion of France (法國) had turned into a desperate retreat. His army, roughly 6,000 men, mostly longbowmen (長弓手), had captured the port of Harfleur but was ravaged by dysentery and marching exhausted toward the safety of English-held Calais (加萊) when a French force of perhaps 20,000 to 25,000 knights and men-at-arms blocked the road near the village of Agincourt (阿金庫爾). Heavy autumn rain had turned the freshly ploughed field between two woods into thick, clinging mud, a detail that would decide the battle.

Henry positioned his exhausted troops in a narrow gap between the woods, protecting his longbowmen behind sharpened wooden stakes driven into the ground at an angle to impale charging horses. When the French knights advanced, first on horseback and then on foot, their heavy plate armour became a trap in the deep mud, which sucked at their boots and left them gasping for breath before they even reached the English line. Longbow arrows, fired in massed volleys from ranges up to 250 metres, could not always pierce plate armour directly but wounded horses and forced attackers into a crushing, disorganised crowd.

Trapped in the narrow killing ground, unable to manoeuvre or even lift their weapons in the crush, thousands of French knights were cut down, trampled, or suffocated in the mud by lightly armoured English soldiers wielding hatchets and knives. English losses totalled a few hundred; French losses are estimated near 6,000, including much of the French nobility, a catastrophe that crippled French military leadership for a generation. The victory, wildly disproportionate to the odds, secured Henry’s reputation as a legendary commander and became the episode later immortalised in Shakespeare’s (莎士比亞) Henry V.

一位東漢學者如何隻身挑戰整個時代的迷信

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
東漢時期，讖緯之學與天人感應之說盛行朝野，一位出身寒微的學者卻寫下數十萬言，逐一駁斥當時盛行的災異、鬼神、命定之說。你認為，這樣一位「異端」學者的著作，在當時會受到甚麼樣的對待？
背景速覽

核心人物：王充（27–約97年，會稽上虞人，東漢思想家）。

核心思想：《論衡》，以「疾虛妄」為宗旨，批判讖緯迷信、天人感應、鬼神禍福之說。

時代背景：東漢讖緯神學與今文經學結合，天人感應、災異譴告之說廣為朝廷與士人所信奉，形成主流意識形態。

東漢疆域圖 — 王充身處的時代背景，讖緯神學與天人感應之說在此時期根深蒂固（王充並無傳世畫像存世）。來源：維基媒體共享資源

王充出身會稽上虞的寒門，家境清貧，早年遊學洛陽太學，因買不起書，常在書肆閱讀，過目成誦。東漢一朝，自漢武帝獨尊儒術以來，逐漸與讖緯神學（以符讖、災異、天人感應解釋政治興衰）結合，形成朝廷與士林深信不疑的主流思想，皇帝施政、災異示警、祥瑞應驗，皆被視為天意與人事互相感應的證據，質疑這套體系近乎質疑皇權本身的正當性。

王充耗費畢生心力撰成《論衡》八十五篇，以「疾虛妄」為核心宗旨，逐一批駁當時流行的虛妄之說：他指出雷擊並非上天譴責惡人，而是自然界陰陽二氣相激而成；批評「感應」之說邏輯不通，質問若真有天人感應，何以善人亦遭橫禍、惡人反享長壽；又駁斥人死為鬼、能禍福生人之說，主張人死氣散，形滅神消，並無鬼魂作祟之理。這些論證建立在樸素的自然觀察與邏輯推理之上，在讖緯瀰漫的時代顯得格格不入。

《論衡》成書後，因觸犯主流思想禁忌，長期未受官方重視，甚至一度被視為異端邪說，王充終生未獲重用，官止州郡小吏，鬱鬱而終。直到東漢末年，蔡邕、王朗等學者輾轉得見此書，驚為奇書，秘藏研讀，《論衡》方才漸為士林所知。近代學者重新評價王充，肯定他是中國思想史上罕見的、以理性精神系統挑戰迷信與神學獨斷的批判性思想家，其懷疑論精神至今仍具啟發意義。

The Philosopher Whose Idea of “Pleasure” Was Mostly About Not Suffering

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Epicurus is often remembered today as the namesake of “epicurean” indulgence, feasting and luxury. Before reading on, what do you think Epicurus himself actually taught about pleasure, and why might his real philosophy have been almost the opposite of his popular reputation?
Context

Key figure: Epicurus (伊比鳩魯, 341–270 BCE), Greek (希臘) philosopher who founded a school known as “the Garden” (花園學派) in Athens (雅典).

Idea at stake: True pleasure (快樂) is not indulgence but ataraxia (心靈寧靜), a tranquil freedom from mental disturbance and bodily pain.

Big picture: Rival schools, especially the Stoics (斯多葛學派), caricatured Epicureanism (伊比鳩魯主義) as crude sensualism, a distortion that stuck for over two thousand years.

A Roman-era bust believed to depict Epicurus (伊比鳩魯), whose “Garden” school in Athens taught that true pleasure meant freedom from anxiety, not indulgence. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Epicurus (伊比鳩魯) founded his school around 306 BCE in a modest garden (花園) on the outskirts of Athens (雅典), admitting students regardless of class or gender, including women and enslaved people, an unusually egalitarian arrangement for the time. In his surviving Letter to Menoeceus (致美諾寇思的信), Epicurus laid out his core ethical claim: pleasure (快樂) is the beginning and end of a happy life, a position that sounds, on its face, exactly like the hedonism his name would later become synonymous with.

But Epicurus’s actual definition of pleasure diverged sharply from indulgence. He distinguished between fleeting, intense sensory pleasures, like rich food or drink, and the deeper, more durable pleasure of ataraxia (心靈寧靜), a stable state of freedom from mental disturbance, paired with aponia (無痛), freedom from bodily pain. He argued that chasing extravagant desires actually generates more suffering than satisfaction, since desire for luxury creates anxiety, dependency, and disappointment, whereas simple pleasures, bread, water, friendship, philosophical conversation, could be enjoyed reliably without such costs.

Rival schools, particularly the Stoics (斯多葛學派) and later early Christian writers, seized on the word “pleasure” and depicted Epicureanism (伊比鳩魯主義) as a philosophy of gluttony and vice, a caricature that proved remarkably durable; the English word “epicurean” still carries connotations of indulgent feasting today, the near-opposite of what Epicurus actually taught. His genuine legacy, a naturalistic ethics built on minimising anxiety and pain rather than maximising sensation, quietly influenced later thinkers from the Enlightenment’s utilitarians (功利主義者) to modern psychological research on the diminishing returns of material consumption.

Why You’d Rather Bet on a Coin Flip Than an Unknown Probability

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Imagine two urns: one has exactly 50 red balls and 50 black balls, the other has 100 red and black balls in an unknown mix. You’re offered an equal cash bet on red from either urn. Before reading on, which urn do you think most people choose to bet on, and why might that reveal something irrational about how humans handle uncertainty?
Daniel Ellsberg (丹尼爾·艾斯伯格) in 1972. His 1961 urn experiment showed people irrationally avoiding bets with unknown odds even over mathematically identical known ones. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In 1961, economist Daniel Ellsberg (丹尼爾·艾斯伯格), later famous for leaking the Pentagon Papers, published a thought experiment exposing a flaw in how classical economics assumed people handle uncertainty. He presented people with two urns: one contained exactly 50 red balls and 50 black balls, known odds; the other contained 100 red and black balls in a completely unknown ratio. Offered an identical bet on drawing red from either urn, most people consistently preferred betting on the known 50-50 urn, even though the true odds of winning were mathematically identical in both cases.

The catch is what made this a genuine paradox: when Ellsberg then asked the same people to bet on black instead of red, they again preferred the known urn over the unknown one, which is logically inconsistent. Preferring “known” for both colours implies believing the unknown urn is simultaneously worse for red and worse for black, an impossibility. Ellsberg labelled this pattern “ambiguity aversion” (模糊規避), and showed it directly violates subjective expected utility theory (主觀期望效用理論), the foundational model of rational choice economists had relied on for decades.

Ambiguity aversion shows up constantly outside the lab: investors often avoid unfamiliar foreign markets even when expected returns look comparable to familiar domestic ones, insurers charge steep premiums for genuinely novel or poorly understood risks, and people prefer doctors who state clear odds over ones who admit deep uncertainty, even when the underlying situations are equally serious. Ellsberg’s 1961 paper helped launch an entire subfield of decision theory studying how people treat “known unknowns” differently from calculable risk, work still actively debated and extended in behavioural economics (行為經濟學) today.

🫀 Physiology

The 3am Charley Horse, and Why It’s Not Really About Dehydration

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Almost everyone has been jolted awake by a sudden, agonising cramp seizing their calf muscle in the middle of the night. Before reading on, what do you think is actually happening inside the muscle to cause this, and is the common advice to drink more water or eat a banana actually backed by evidence?
The gastrocnemius (腓腸肌), the calf muscle most often seized by nocturnal leg cramps. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Nocturnal leg cramps (夜間腿抽筋), sudden, involuntary, and often excruciating contractions usually striking the calf (腓腸肌) or foot during sleep, affect a large share of adults, with prevalence rising sharply after age 50 and in pregnancy. For decades, the dominant public explanation blamed dehydration or electrolyte imbalances, particularly low potassium (鉀) or magnesium (鎂), an appealingly simple story that shaped decades of “drink more water, eat a banana” advice. Clinical research, however, has repeatedly failed to find consistent differences in hydration or blood electrolyte levels between people who get frequent cramps and those who don’t.

A more consistent explanation, first proposed by researcher Martin Schwellnus (施韋爾努斯) in the late 1990s, points instead to disrupted communication within the nervous system controlling the muscle. Cramps appear to arise when local muscle fatigue upsets the normal balance between excitatory signals from stretch-sensing receptors called muscle spindles (肌梭), which tell a muscle to contract, and inhibitory signals from Golgi tendon organs (高爾基腱器官), which normally tell it to relax under tension. When inhibition weakens relative to excitation, the spinal reflex arc controlling the muscle essentially gets stuck in an over-excited loop, locking it into sustained, painful contraction.

This neuromuscular theory explains why stretching the cramping muscle, gently pulling the toes upward to lengthen the calf, reliably and rapidly ends an attack: the stretch directly reactivates the Golgi tendon organ’s inhibitory signal, overriding the runaway excitatory loop. It also explains why cramps cluster in people whose muscles are fatigued, deconditioned, or held in shortened positions for long periods, rather than in people who happen to be mildly dehydrated. Researchers caution the mechanism still isn’t fully settled, and persistent, severe cramps warrant a doctor’s evaluation to rule out nerve or circulatory conditions.

Does Drinking Collagen Powder Actually Firm Up Your Skin?

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Collagen powders and drinks have become one of the best-selling supplement categories worldwide, marketed to improve skin elasticity and reduce wrinkles. Before reading on, what do you think happens to collagen protein once you swallow it, and does the evidence actually support the skin-firming claims?
Gelatin (明膠), derived from collagen (膠原蛋白) through partial breakdown — the same protein family marketed in hydrolyzed collagen supplements. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Collagen (膠原蛋白) is the most abundant structural protein in the human body, forming much of the scaffolding in skin (皮膚), tendons, and cartilage, and natural production declines with age, a key driver of thinning, less elastic skin. Supplement companies market hydrolyzed collagen (水解膠原蛋白), collagen broken into smaller peptide fragments for easier absorption, claiming oral consumption directly rebuilds skin structure. Sceptics initially dismissed this as implausible: digestion breaks nearly all dietary protein down into individual amino acids (氨基酸) and small peptides, seemingly no different from eating any other protein source like meat or gelatin (明膠).

Despite that plausible objection, a substantial body of clinical evidence has accumulated testing this specific claim directly. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis by Miranda (米蘭達) and colleagues, pooling 19 randomised trials and over 1,100 participants, found oral hydrolyzed collagen supplementation, typically 2.5 to 10 grams daily for 8 to 24 weeks, produced statistically significant improvements in skin hydration, elasticity, and wrinkle reduction compared to placebo. The proposed mechanism isn’t collagen fragments being deposited directly into skin, but specific collagen-derived peptides acting as signalling molecules that stimulate the body’s own fibroblasts (成纖維細胞) to produce more collagen and hyaluronic acid (透明質酸).

The evidence has real limits worth noting: most trials were funded by supplement manufacturers, nearly all participants were women, effect sizes, while statistically real, are modest rather than dramatic, and there’s no reliable way to compare outcomes between different collagen sources or doses. Collagen supplementation appears to be one of the rarer cases where a popular wellness trend has genuine, if modest, clinical support behind it, though it remains a slow, incremental effect measured in gradually improved hydration and elasticity over months, not a fast fix for visible wrinkles.

🏋 Sports Science

How Lifting Light Weights With a Strap Around Your Leg Builds Real Muscle

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Blood flow restriction (BFR) training involves wrapping a pressure cuff or strap around the top of an arm or leg while lifting surprisingly light weights, sometimes just 20-30% of a person’s usual maximum. Before reading on, what do you think restricting blood flow during exercise actually does to the muscle, and can it really build strength comparable to heavy lifting?
Blood flow restriction training pairs light loads, often 20–30% of a normal working weight, with a pressure cuff to trigger muscle growth through metabolic stress. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Blood flow restriction (血流限制) training, sometimes called occlusion training or by its Japanese trade name Kaatsu (加壓訓練), involves wrapping an inflatable cuff around the top of a limb during exercise, tight enough to significantly restrict venous blood flow leaving the muscle while still allowing arterial blood in. Practitioners then perform resistance exercises at unusually low loads, often just 20 to 30% of a person’s one-repetition maximum, far below the roughly 70% intensity normally considered necessary to build meaningful muscle and strength through conventional training.

Despite the light loads, multiple meta-analyses, including work by researcher Jeremy Loenneke (勒尼克), have confirmed BFR training produces muscle growth and strength gains comparable to much heavier conventional resistance training. The mechanism differs fundamentally from traditional lifting: rather than relying primarily on mechanical tension from heavy loads, restricted blood flow creates a build-up of metabolic byproducts, lactate (乳酸) and reactive oxygen species, inside the working muscle, triggering release of growth hormone (生長激素) and activating the mTOR signalling pathway that drives protein synthesis, alongside faster recruitment of fast-twitch muscle fibres than light loading alone would normally cause.

This makes BFR training especially valuable in rehabilitation settings, where patients recovering from injury or surgery often cannot tolerate heavy loading on a joint but still need to prevent muscle atrophy (肌肉萎縮); physical therapists now use it after ACL reconstruction and in older adults with limited joint tolerance. It isn’t risk-free: incorrect cuff pressure, use over inappropriate durations, or application in people with clotting disorders or vascular disease can cause harm, so proper training and pressure calibration, ideally under supervision, matters more with BFR than with most conventional exercise methods.

📈 Investing & Economic Thinking

The Stock Bubble So Wild It Fooled the Man Who Discovered Gravity

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In 1720, one of history’s greatest scientific minds, a man who had already worked out the laws of gravity and motion, bought and sold shares in a wildly speculative company not once but twice. Before reading on, what do you think happened the second time he invested, and what famous line did he reportedly say about the whole affair?
Sir Isaac Newton (艾薩克·牛頓), whose scientific genius offered no protection against the herd psychology of the 1720 South Sea Bubble (南海泡沫). Credit: Wikimedia Commons

The South Sea Company (南海公司) was founded in 1711, granted a monopoly on British trade with Spanish (西班牙) South America in exchange for absorbing a large portion of British government war debt, a business model that depended almost entirely on future trade privileges that never really materialised at meaningful scale. By early 1720, speculative fever had seized London (倫敦): the company’s stock price, driven by rumour, bribed politicians, and aggressive promotion rather than actual profits, rocketed from around £128 in January to over £1,000 by August, dragging in investors from every level of society.

Sir Isaac Newton (艾薩克·牛頓), already world-famous for the laws of motion and universal gravitation, owned South Sea shares early in the rally and, sensing the mania was overheating, sold in April 1720, pocketing roughly a 100% profit of about £7,000. But as the stock kept climbing through the summer, Newton watched friends and acquaintances grow spectacularly richer and, swept up by the same enthusiasm he’d initially resisted, bought back in near the peak. When the bubble finally collapsed in September 1720, Newton lost roughly £20,000, several times his annual income as Master of the Mint, reportedly remarking that he “could calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people.”

The collapse triggered a genuine financial crisis, ruining investors across British society and prompting Parliament to pass the Bubble Act (泡沫法案) restricting the formation of new joint-stock companies for over a century, a regulatory overreaction that likely slowed legitimate British business formation for generations. Newton’s story endures in financial history precisely because it demonstrates that raw intelligence offers no protection against herd psychology and market mania; understanding orbital mechanics perfectly did nothing to help him resist watching everyone around him seemingly get rich, then panic-buying near the top exactly as an amateur investor might.

⚖️ Political Economics — Theory in the Wild

The Growth Ceiling That Traps Countries Just Short of Rich

Growth that carries a country from poor to middle-income doesn’t reliably continue to rich-country status — the puzzle behind the “middle income trap” (中等收入陷阱). Credit: Wikimedia Commons
🌍 Event
Since the 2000s, fast-growing economies including Malaysia (馬來西亞), Thailand (泰國), and Brazil (巴西) have seen growth stall for years after reaching upper-middle-income status, unable to break through to advanced-economy income levels despite decades of earlier rapid catch-up growth.
📖 Theory
Standard growth economics predicts convergence (趨同): poorer countries, by importing technology and moving low-cost labour into manufacturing, should keep growing faster than rich countries until incomes equalise, the process that powered East Asia’s post-war “growth miracles.”
⚠️ Anomaly
World Bank economists Indermit Gill (吉爾) and Homi Kharas (卡拉斯) coined the term “middle income trap” (中等收入陷阱) in 2007 after observing many countries stall for decades around $10,000–$12,000 per capita income, squeezed between low-wage competitors and rich-country innovators.
🧪 Challenger
Institutional economists including Daron Acemoglu (阿西莫格魯) argue the missing ingredient isn’t capital but “inclusive” institutions enabling genuine innovation rather than imitation; whether China (中國), near this threshold, escapes through innovation or stalls remains an open question.
💡 Getting a country from poor to middle-income and from middle-income to rich appear to require two entirely different sets of economic ingredients.
📖 Gill, I. & Kharas, H. (2007), An East Asian Renaissance, World Bank. Acemoglu, D. & Robinson, J.A. (2012), Why Nations Fail.

🔗 Connection of the Day

Cards 6 & 10 — When Being Rational Isn’t Enough: Daniel Ellsberg’s 1961 urn experiment showed ordinary people making mathematically inconsistent choices when faced with ambiguity, avoiding unknown odds even when the true probabilities were identical to a known bet. Isaac Newton, one of history’s most rigorous mathematical minds, fell prey to an even starker version of the same failure: he could calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies precisely, yet still panic-bought South Sea stock near its peak after watching others get rich, losing a fortune. Both show that logical intelligence and psychological bias run on separate tracks; knowing the math doesn’t inoculate anyone against herd behaviour or discomfort with uncertainty.

So what: Before a big financial or personal decision, ask not just “what do the numbers say” but “am I being pulled by what everyone else is doing, or by discomfort with not knowing the odds” — intellect alone couldn’t save Ellsberg’s subjects or Newton from that pull.
