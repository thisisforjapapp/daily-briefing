🔭 Astronomy & Cosmos

Webb’s First Snapshot of a World Beyond Our Solar System

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In September 2022, astronomers pointed the newest and most powerful space telescope ever built at a star 385 light-years away and captured something no instrument had ever captured before at that wavelength of light. Before reading on, what do you think made this image so difficult to take, and why did scientists need a special trick just to see the planet at all?
HIP 65426 b, a gas-giant exoplanet (系外行星) roughly 385 light-years away — in 2022 the James Webb Space Telescope (詹姆斯·韋伯太空望遠鏡) captured it directly at infrared wavelengths no prior instrument could reach. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

On 1 September 2022, NASA (美國太空總署) released the James Webb Space Telescope’s (詹姆斯·韋伯太空望遠鏡) first direct image of a planet beyond our solar system: HIP 65426 b, a Jupiter-like gas giant orbiting a young star roughly 385 light-years away. First discovered in 2017 by the European Southern Observatory’s (歐洲南方天文台) Very Large Telescope in Chile using ground-based infrared imaging, the planet had never before been captured at wavelengths longer than 5 microns, a range only Webb’s instruments could reach. The new image showed the same faint point of light in four different infrared colours, layered together like a set of fingerprints.

Direct imaging of any exoplanet (系外行星) is extraordinarily difficult: a planet is typically millions to billions of times fainter than the star it orbits, so its light is normally drowned out completely. Webb’s cameras, the Near-Infrared Camera (近紅外相機, NIRCam) and Mid-Infrared Instrument (中紅外儀, MIRI), both carry coronagraphs (日冕儀), tiny masks engineered to block a star’s overwhelming glare while letting the much dimmer planetary light through. HIP 65426 b orbits roughly 100 times farther from its star than Earth does from the Sun, a wide separation that made it an ideal early target for testing this technique.

The image itself is unglamorous, a faint blob rather than a detailed world, but its significance lies in proving the method works: Webb could now directly detect planetary light at wavelengths ground-based telescopes and even Hubble (哈勃太空望遠鏡) could not reach, opening a path toward eventually photographing smaller, cooler, more Earth-like planets. Scientists have since applied the same coronagraph technique to dozens of other systems, edging toward the long-term goal of directly imaging a potentially habitable world.

一位國王如何親自為百姓發明一套全新文字

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
十五世紀朝鮮，絕大多數平民百姓不識漢字，無法讀寫。一位國王卻決心親自主持創製一套全新的文字系統。你認為，這套文字最終為何能流傳至今，成為今日韓國的官方文字？
背景速覽

核心人物：世宗大王（李祹，1397–1450，朝鮮王朝第四代國王）；集賢殿學者鄭麟趾、申叔舟、成三問等人。

事件：1443年冬創製訓民正音，1446年正式頒布《訓民正音》解例本。

時代背景：朝鮮沿用漢字書寫典籍公文，漢字筆畫繁複，非長年苦讀不能掌握，平民百姓普遍不識字，難以表達訴求或閱讀律令。

世宗大王的官方標準畫像 — 他於1443年親自主持創製訓民正音，即今日韓文的前身。來源：維基媒體共享資源

世宗大王李祹自幼好學，即位後推行多項文治，設集賢殿延攬學者研習經史曆算。當時朝鮮官方文書、典籍一律使用漢字書寫，漢字結構複雜，須長年苦讀方能通曉，尋常百姓終生不識字，遇官司訴訟、田契買賣，往往無法親自書寫，只能仰賴識字者代筆，甚或因此蒙受冤屈。世宗深感文字之隔閡阻礙政令下達與民情上達，遂萌生創製一套易學易用文字的念頭。

世宗與集賢殿學者鄭麟趾、申叔舟、成三問等人秘密研究數年，依據人類發音器官形狀（舌、齒、喉）設計子音字母，又取「天、地、人」三才哲學概念設計母音字母，於1443年冬完成初稿，定名「訓民正音」，意即「教導百姓正確字音」。這套文字僅得二十八個字母，筆畫簡單、組合靈活，識字者往往只需數日便能掌握基本拼讀，與漢字動輒數千字、須經年累月背誦的難度形成強烈對比。

1446年，世宗正式頒布《訓民正音》解例本，詳述創製原理與拼寫規則，但這套文字初期遭到部分儒臣崔萬理等人強烈反對，斥為「捨中國而就夷狄」，恐動搖漢字士大夫的文化地位，因此長期未能取代漢字成為官方主要文字，多用於婦女、平民及佛經翻譯。直至十九世紀末至二十世紀，訓民正音才逐漸普及，正式定名「韓文」，成為今日韓國家喻戶曉、全民通用的官方文字系統。

🏛 History — Western

The Arrow, the Shield Wall, and the End of Anglo-Saxon England

🤔 Predict Before You Read
On 14 October 1066, an exhausted English army that had just force-marched hundreds of kilometres after winning one battle faced a fresh Norman invasion force on a hill in southern England. Before reading on, what do you think finally broke the famously disciplined English shield wall, and why did this single day’s fighting reshape English language and culture for centuries?
Context

Key figures: King Harold II (哈羅德二世) of England (英格蘭); William, Duke of Normandy (諾曼第公爵威廉), later William the Conqueror (征服者威廉).

Event at stake: The Battle of Hastings (黑斯廷斯戰役), 14 October 1066, deciding the English throne.

Big picture: Harold had just marched his army roughly 400 km to crush a Norwegian invasion, then rushed south to meet a second invasion before his exhausted troops could recover.

A scene from the Bayeux Tapestry (貝葉掛毯) depicting the death of King Harold at the Battle of Hastings, 14 October 1066. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

When King Edward the Confessor (懺悔者愛德華) died childless in January 1066, the throne passed to the powerful nobleman Harold Godwinson, crowned Harold II. Two rivals disputed his claim: Norway’s (挪威) King Harald Hardrada invaded in the north, while William, Duke of Normandy, claimed Edward had promised him the crown years earlier. Harold marched his army roughly 400 kilometres north in under two weeks, crushed Hardrada’s Norwegian force at the Battle of Stamford Bridge (斯坦福橋戰役) on 25 September, then immediately turned south again on hearing William had landed at Pevensey (佩文西).

By 14 October, Harold’s exhausted troops, many of them half-armed peasant levies, took up position on Senlac Hill near Hastings, forming the traditional English shield wall (盾牆), a tight, disciplined line of overlapping shields that had repelled cavalry charges for generations. William’s Norman force, roughly matched in size but including cavalry and archers Harold lacked, repeatedly charged the hill and repeatedly failed to break the wall. According to the near-contemporary Bayeux Tapestry (貝葉掛毯), a woven account of the battle, the Normans eventually staged a series of feigned retreats, luring undisciplined sections of the English line down the hill in pursuit, where they could be cut off and destroyed.

As the shield wall thinned and gaps opened, Norman cavalry and archers pressed the advantage; Harold himself was killed late in the battle, traditionally said to have been struck in the eye by an arrow, though the exact circumstances remain debated among historians. With Harold dead and his army broken, English resistance collapsed; William marched on London (倫敦) and was crowned king on Christmas Day 1066, ending nearly 500 years of Anglo-Saxon rule. The Norman Conquest (諾曼征服) reshaped English law, land ownership, and language, embedding thousands of French words into English within a few generations.

一位名士臨刑前彈奏的最後一曲，為何成為千古絕響

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
三國魏末，一位才華橫溢、崇尚老莊、藐視禮法的名士，因仗義執言為友人辯護，觸怒當權者而被判處死刑。你認為，他在刑場之上，會做出甚麼樣的最後舉動？
背景速覽

核心人物：嵇康（223–263，三國魏末思想家、文學家、音樂家，竹林七賢精神領袖）；司馬昭（魏權臣）。

核心思想：崇尚老莊自然之道，主張「越名教而任自然」，以清談玄理、寄情山水琴酒對抗禮法名教的虛偽。

時代背景：魏末司馬氏專權，篡奪曹魏政權野心昭然，士人動輒因言獲罪，嵇康與阮籍、山濤、向秀、劉伶、阮咸、王戎常聚竹林縱酒清談，史稱「竹林七賢」。

「竹林七聖圖」，描繪嵇康與阮籍等七位名士聚於竹林縱酒清談的傳統畫題。來源：維基媒體共享資源

嵇康出身官宦之家，娶曹魏宗室之女為妻，博學多才，通曉音律，尤擅古琴，性情孤高，不慕榮利，屢辭朝廷徵召。當時司馬氏權傾朝野，篡魏之心路人皆知，朝中士人為求自保，多依附司馬氏或明哲保身，嵇康卻與阮籍等志同道合之士常聚於山陽竹林之中，飲酒賦詩、彈琴清談，議論老莊玄理，倡言「越名教而任自然」，公然蔑視司馬氏所標榜的名教禮法，成為竹林七賢的精神領袖。

景元四年（263年），嵇康好友呂安遭其兄呂巽誣告不孝下獄，嵇康仗義執言，出面為呂安辯白作證，此舉觸怒了正欲以名教禮法籠絡人心、鞏固權力的司馬昭。素與嵇康有隙的鍾會趁機在司馬昭面前進讒，指嵇康「言論放蕩，非毀典謨」，恐其成為反對司馬氏篡魏的精神旗幟，足以動搖人心。司馬昭遂下令將嵇康與呂安一併處死，罪名雖是不孝之獄的牽連，實則是剷除異己、殺一儆百的政治決斷。

嵇康臨刑之際，神色自若，索琴彈奏一曲《廣陵散》，曲罷長嘆：「昔袁孝尼嘗從吾學《廣陵散》，吾每靳固之，《廣陵散》於今絕矣！」從容就戮，年僅四十歲。此事經《世說新語》記載流傳千古，「廣陵散絕」遂成為士人風骨與文化絕響的代名詞。竹林七賢自此分崩離析，阮籍佯狂避禍，山濤、王戎轉而出仕司馬氏政權，唯嵇康以死明志，其「越名教而任自然」的思想，成為魏晉玄學留給後世最富反抗精神的一頁。

🌿 Philosophy — Western

The Philosopher Who Said the Only Serious Question Is Whether Life Is Worth Living

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In 1942, a young French-Algerian writer opened a philosophical essay with the claim that suicide is the one truly serious philosophical problem, since everything else comes after that. Before reading on, what conclusion do you think he reached about whether life is worth living in a universe with no inherent meaning?
Context

Key figure: Albert Camus (阿爾貝·卡繆, 1913–1960), French-Algerian (法屬阿爾及利亞) writer and philosopher.

Idea at stake: The Absurd (荒謬) — the collision between humanity’s demand for meaning and the universe’s indifferent silence — and whether life remains worth living once that meaning is absent.

Big picture: Written during the German occupation of France, Camus’s essay wrestled with despair and nihilism without surrendering to either suicide or false hope.

Albert Camus, whose 1942 essay The Myth of Sisyphus argued life remains worth living even without inherent cosmic meaning. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Camus published The Myth of Sisyphus (薛西弗斯的神話) in 1942, opening with a deliberately provocative claim: “There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide.” He argued that human beings crave meaning, coherence, and purpose from existence, while the universe itself offers only indifferent silence in return; this collision, not any single event or argument, is what he called the Absurd. Rather than resolving the tension by inventing false comfort, whether religious faith or philosophical systems promising hidden order, Camus insisted the honest response was to hold both truths simultaneously without blinking.

To illustrate his point, Camus turned to the Greek myth of Sisyphus (薛西弗斯), a king condemned by the gods to eternally push a boulder up a mountain, only to watch it roll back down each time he neared the top, forever, with no possibility of completion or reward. Most readers assumed this fate represented pure despair, but Camus argued the opposite: Sisyphus becomes genuinely free the moment he stops hoping the boulder will stay at the summit and instead fully inhabits the struggle itself, scorning the gods’ punishment by refusing to be crushed by its pointlessness. Camus’s famous closing line, “one must imagine Sisyphus happy,” reframed absurd, repetitive labour as a site of dignity rather than tragedy.

Camus rejected the label “existentialist” (存在主義者), preferring to distinguish his Absurdism from Jean-Paul Sartre’s (讓-保羅·沙特) philosophy, though the two moved in overlapping Parisian intellectual circles before a bitter public falling-out over politics in the 1950s. Camus was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature (諾貝爾文學獎) in 1957 at just 44, one of the youngest laureates ever, for work the committee praised for illuminating “the problems of the human conscience.” He died just over two years later, on 4 January 1960, in a car crash outside Paris, an ending some later called absurd in exactly the sense he had spent his career describing.

🧠 Psychology

The Doomsday Cult That Got More Convinced After the World Didn’t End

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In the 1950s, a small group of believers convinced their leader was receiving messages from aliens preparing to evacuate them before a catastrophic flood destroyed the world on a specific date. A psychologist secretly joined the group to watch what happened when that date came and went with nothing happening at all. Before reading on, what do you think the believers did after their prophecy visibly failed?
Leon Festinger (利昂·費斯廷格), who secretly infiltrated a doomsday cult in 1954 to observe what happens when a prophecy visibly fails. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In 1954, researcher Leon Festinger (利昂·費斯廷格) learned of a small Chicago group calling themselves the Seekers (探索者), led by a woman who claimed to receive messages from extraterrestrial beings warning of an imminent, catastrophic flood, with a spaceship promised to rescue true believers just beforehand on a specific date. Recognising a rare opportunity to observe belief under direct empirical pressure, Festinger and colleagues Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter secretly infiltrated the group as ordinary members, documenting everything as the predicted date approached and members quit jobs, sold possessions, and waited.

When the appointed night arrived and passed with no flood, no spaceship, and no message from beyond, Festinger predicted the group would not simply admit error and disperse. Members who had made minimal public commitment quietly drifted away embarrassed, but the group’s most devoted members, those who had sacrificed the most, jobs, money, relationships, responded by finding a face-saving explanation: their faith and vigil had convinced a higher power to spare the world after all. Rather than abandoning the belief, many of the most invested Seekers began proselytising more aggressively than before, actively recruiting new believers to help resolve their internal discomfort.

Festinger explained this pattern the following year in A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance (認知失調理論, 1957): when a person holds two contradictory beliefs, or when reality contradicts a costly prior commitment, the resulting psychological discomfort pushes people to reduce it, often not by changing the belief but by changing how they interpret the evidence, especially when abandoning the belief would mean admitting a very public and costly mistake. The theory has since been used to explain everything from why smokers dismiss health warnings to why partisans reinterpret failed predictions, though recent historical reviews have questioned some details of the original Seekers account.

🫀 Physiology

Why a Heart Attack Can Feel Like Pain in Your Left Arm

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Doctors have long known that a heart attack often doesn’t just hurt in the chest, it can radiate pain down the left arm, into the jaw, or even into the back, nowhere near the heart itself. Before reading on, what do you think is happening inside the nervous system to make pain from an internal organ feel like it’s coming from somewhere else entirely?
A referred pain (轉移痛) chart mapping how pain from internal organs is felt on the skin surface, including the heart’s classic radiation into the left arm. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Referred pain (轉移痛) is the phenomenon where pain originating in an internal organ, like the heart, gallbladder, or appendix, is perceived by the brain as coming from a different location entirely, usually a patch of skin or muscle nowhere near the actual source. The clearest everyday example is cardiac pain: during a heart attack (心肌梗塞), many patients feel a crushing sensation not just in the chest but radiating down the inside of the left arm, into the neck, or up into the jaw, even though nothing is actually wrong with the arm or jaw tissue itself.

The mechanism traces back to how nerve signals are wired in the spinal cord (脊髓). Sensory nerve fibres from internal organs and sensory nerve fibres from a specific patch of skin, called a dermatome (皮節), both converge onto the same relay neurons in the spinal cord before travelling up to the brain. Because the brain receives vastly more everyday input from skin than from internal organs, it has effectively learned to interpret ambiguous signals arriving at that shared relay point as coming from the skin, the more common and better-mapped source, rather than correctly tracing them back to the organ where the pain actually began.

For the heart specifically, cardiac sensory nerves enter the spinal cord at roughly the same segments, T1 through T5, that also receive sensory input from the chest and the inner surface of the left arm, which is precisely why cardiac pain characteristically radiates there rather than to the leg or the right hand. This convergence isn’t a flaw so much as a byproduct of an efficient, shared communication pathway, but it has real diagnostic value: doctors use well-mapped referred pain patterns, gallbladder pain to the right shoulder blade, kidney stones to the groin, as genuine clinical clues pointing toward an underlying organ even before imaging confirms it.

Does Sugar Actually Make Kids Bounce Off the Walls?

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Birthday parties, Halloween, holiday candy, generations of parents have blamed sugar for turning calm children into hyperactive whirlwinds. Before reading on, what do you think decades of controlled scientific trials actually found when researchers tested sugar’s effect on children’s behaviour directly, and why might the belief persist so strongly even if the science says otherwise?
Refined sugar (糖) — decades of double-blind trials have found no effect on children’s behaviour, despite the popular belief. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

The idea that sugar (糖) causes hyperactivity in children has been a fixture of parenting folklore for decades, reinforced by countless birthday parties where sugary cake seems to precede chaos. The claim traces back partly to a 1970s hypothesis by allergist Benjamin Feingold, who proposed sugar and food additives caused hyperactive behaviour, sparking widespread public concern and dietary restriction movements even though his original claims were never rigorously tested against a placebo.

In 1995, researcher Mark Wolraich (沃萊克) and colleagues published a landmark meta-analysis in the Journal of the American Medical Association, pooling results from 23 separate double-blind, randomised controlled trials involving over 1,400 children. In these carefully designed studies, neither the children nor the observers rating their behaviour knew whether a given child had actually consumed sugar or a sugar-free substitute, eliminating expectation bias from the measurement itself. The conclusion was unambiguous: sugar did not affect children’s behaviour or cognitive performance, a finding that held even among children whose parents specifically described them as “sugar-sensitive.”

One particularly revealing follow-up study explains why the myth persists so stubbornly: researchers gave all children in a group the identical sugar-free drink, but told half the mothers their child had just consumed a large dose of sugar. Mothers who believed their child had eaten sugar rated them as significantly more hyperactive immediately afterward, even though no child in the study had consumed any sugar at all. The real driver, researchers concluded, is parental expectation bias combined with the naturally high-energy, excitable behaviour typical of birthday parties and holidays, occasions when sugar and stimulating environments simply happen to coincide.

The Slow, Easy Cardio Pace Elite Endurance Athletes Swear By

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Elite cyclists and marathoners often spend 70 to 80 percent of their training time at a deceptively easy, conversational pace, not sprinting, not even breathing hard, rather than training at maximum intensity as often as possible. Before reading on, what do you think this slow, low-intensity training actually does inside the body, and is the popular claim about it actually settled science?
Steady, conversational-pace aerobic exercise (有氧運動) — the intensity elite endurance programmes build the bulk of their training volume around. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Zone 2 training (第二區間訓練) refers to exercising at a low-to-moderate intensity, roughly 60 to 70% of maximum heart rate, a pace easy enough to hold a conversation but sustained for extended periods, often 45 minutes to several hours. The approach gained mainstream popularity partly through the work of physiologist Iñigo San-Millán (聖米蘭), who studied professional cyclists including Tour de France (環法自行車賽) competitors and argued this specific intensity uniquely stimulates mitochondrial biogenesis (粒線體生合成), the process by which cells build new mitochondria, the structures that convert fuel into usable energy.

San-Millán’s argument, drawn from decades of testing elite athletes and metabolic disease patients, holds that Zone 2 intensity maximises the muscle’s reliance on fat oxidation while minimising lactate (乳酸) production, conditions he claims specifically favour building aerobic, mitochondria-rich muscle fibre over time, improving an athlete’s ability to sustain effort at higher speeds later. Elite endurance programmes across cycling, running, and triathlon widely adopted 80/20 training splits, roughly 80% low intensity and 20% high intensity, partly influenced by this framework.

The claim isn’t without controversy: a 2026 narrative review found the evidence for Zone 2 specifically, as opposed to moderate-to-vigorous exercise generally, driving superior mitochondrial adaptation in the general population was weaker than popular fitness media suggests, with some researchers arguing higher intensities produce comparable or greater fitness gains in ordinary exercisers. What remains uncontested is that sustained low-intensity aerobic work builds a genuine fitness base and allows far greater weekly training volume without excessive fatigue or injury risk, valuable on its own even if the specific mitochondrial mechanism remains debated.

The Energy Giant That Hid Its Losses in Companies Named After Star Wars

🤔 Predict Before You Read
By 2000, one company was named “America’s Most Innovative Company” by Fortune magazine for six consecutive years, its stock soaring, its executives celebrated as visionaries. Within about a year, it would file the largest bankruptcy in American history at the time. Before reading on, what do you think this company was actually doing behind the scenes to make its finances look so much healthier than they really were?
Enron’s (安隆) corporate logo — once celebrated as America’s most innovative company, it collapsed in 2001 in what was then the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Enron (安隆), a Houston-based energy trading company, was celebrated throughout the late 1990s as a symbol of American corporate innovation, named Fortune magazine’s “Most Innovative Company” six years running while its stock price and executive reputations soared. Behind the scenes, chief financial officer Andrew Fastow (安德魯·法斯托) had built an elaborate network of off-the-books partnerships called special purpose entities (特殊目的實體, SPEs), some whimsically named after Star Wars characters like “Chewco” and “Jedi,” designed to absorb Enron’s troubled assets and debts, keeping them hidden from the company’s official balance sheet.

By moving underperforming investments and mounting liabilities into these SPEs, Enron could report inflated profits and understated debt to shareholders and regulators, while Fastow personally profited from managing several of the very partnerships he had created, a blatant conflict of interest largely concealed from Enron’s board. Enron’s outside auditor, the prestigious accounting firm Arthur Andersen (安達信會計師事務所), which also earned lucrative consulting fees from Enron, either missed or overlooked the scheme for years, a failure that later destroyed the firm’s own credibility and existence.

The house of cards began collapsing in October 2001 when Enron restated its earnings, revealing hundreds of millions in previously hidden losses; the stock, once trading above $90, fell to under a dollar within weeks. Enron filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on 2 December 2001 with $63.4 billion in assets, then the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history, wiping out thousands of employees’ retirement savings, which had been heavily invested in Enron stock. The scandal directly led to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act (沙賓法案) of 2002, overhauling corporate financial reporting and auditor independence requirements across American business.

⚖️ Political Economics — Theory in the Wild

Why Shared Resources Don’t Always Get Destroyed

Unregulated factory smoke — a classic illustration of the “tragedy of the commons” (公地悲劇), the shared-resource problem Elinor Ostrom (埃莉諾·奧斯特羅姆) showed communities could solve without privatization or the state. Credit: Wikimedia Commons
🌍 Event
For centuries, communities from Swiss Alpine villages to Maine lobster fisheries have shared grazing land, irrigation water, or fishing grounds without collapsing into overuse, directly at odds with predictions that shared resources are doomed to be depleted.
📖 Theory
Ecologist Garrett Hardin’s 1968 essay “The Tragedy of the Commons” argued any resource open to unrestricted shared use will inevitably be overexploited, since each user gains fully from extra use while costs spread across everyone, making privatization or state control seem like the only solutions.
⚠️ Anomaly
Political economist Elinor Ostrom spent decades documenting real-world commons, pastures, irrigation systems, fisheries, sustainably managed for generations by their own communities, without privatization or top-down regulation, directly contradicting Hardin’s supposedly inevitable tragedy.
🧪 Challenger
In her 1990 book Governing the Commons, Ostrom identified institutional design principles, clear boundaries, locally adapted rules, graduated sanctions, low-cost conflict resolution, letting communities self-govern shared resources successfully; she won the 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, the first woman to do so.
💡 The “tragedy” of shared resources isn’t inevitable — well-designed local rules can make communities better custodians than either private owners or the state.
📖 Hardin, G. (1968), Science, “The Tragedy of the Commons.” Ostrom, E. (1990), Governing the Commons. Nobel Prize committee citation (2009).

🔗 Connection of the Day

Cards 6 & 10 — Doubling Down Instead of Admitting Failure: Festinger’s Seekers, faced with a flood and spaceship that never arrived, didn’t quit and admit they were wrong; the most invested members recruited harder, reinterpreting the failure as proof their faith had actually worked. Enron’s leadership, faced with a business model that was quietly failing to generate the profits it claimed, didn’t stop and admit the problem either; they built ever more elaborate off-books partnerships to keep the story alive rather than confess the truth to shareholders. Both cases show that the more costly a wrong belief becomes to abandon, the more elaborate the self-deception required to protect it.

So what: Before doubling down on a decision that isn’t working, whether a relationship, an investment, or a project, ask honestly whether you’re protecting the truth or protecting your own past investment in being right. The size of your prior commitment is not evidence that the original belief was correct.
