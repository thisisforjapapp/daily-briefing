🔭 Astronomy & Cosmos

The First Photograph Ever Taken of a Black Hole

🤔 Predict Before You Read
A black hole's gravity is so strong that not even light can escape it — which means, strictly speaking, it cannot be photographed. In 2019 scientists announced they had done exactly that anyway. Before reading on, what do you think an image of something fundamentally invisible could actually show?
The Event Horizon Telescope (事件視界望遠鏡) collaboration's image, released 10 April 2019, testing general relativity's predictions for a black hole's shadow. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

On 10 April 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope (事件視界望遠鏡, EHT) collaboration released the first image ever taken of a black hole (黑洞): a glowing orange ring wrapped around a dark void, at the centre of the galaxy Messier 87 (M87星系), roughly 55 million light-years away. The object, catalogued as M87* and estimated at 6.5 billion solar masses, cannot literally be photographed, since no light escapes a black hole's event horizon (事件視界). What the image actually captures is the shadow the black hole casts on superheated plasma swirling just outside that boundary, bent into a ring by gravity so extreme it curves the plasma's own light around behind the hole.

No single telescope could resolve something this small and distant — comparable to spotting an orange on the Moon from Earth. The EHT team solved this by linking eight radio observatories (電波望遠鏡) worldwide, from Hawaii to Antarctica to Spain, synchronised with atomic clocks accurate to fractions of a second. Combined via very-long-baseline interferometry (特長基線干涉測量法), the network behaved as one dish roughly Earth's diameter. Reconstructing an image from the scattered data took two years and several independent algorithms, one built by then-graduate student Katie Bouman (凱蒂·鮑曼), who became an unlikely public face of the project.

The ring's size and asymmetry matched Einstein's general relativity (廣義相對論) almost exactly, since gravity that strong bends light more on one side of the disc than the other, brightening it. In 2022 the same collaboration imaged Sagittarius A* (人馬座A*), the far smaller black hole at the Milky Way's own centre; despite being a thousand times less massive than M87*, it appears a similar angular size from Earth simply because it is a thousand times closer. Both images remain the only direct visual evidence, rather than indirect inference, that black holes' predicted shadows really look the way theory said they would.

鄭和下西洋——明成祖如何用艦隊代替奏摺，向世界宣示國威

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
1402年，明成祖朱棣從姪兒建文帝手中奪得帝位，建文帝卻在城破後下落不明。三年後，成祖派遣親信宦官率領二百餘艘艦船、逾兩萬七千人的龐大艦隊出海遠航。你認為，這支明朝史上規模空前的艦隊，除了宣揚國威、招徠朝貢之外，還可能背負著甚麼不便明言的任務？
背景速覽

核心人物：鄭和（1371–1433年，本姓馬，雲南回族，明成祖近侍宦官）；明成祖朱棣（1360–1424年，明朝第三位皇帝）。

事件：永樂三年（1405年）起，鄭和奉命七次率龐大艦隊出使「西洋」，遠達東南亞、印度、阿拉伯半島與東非。

時代背景：朱棣以「靖難之役」奪位，建文帝下落不明，朝廷急需鞏固統治正當性並向四方宣示國威；明初國力鼎盛，具備建造龐大艦隊的財政與技術基礎。

位於馬六甲的鄭和紀念雕像，紀念這位率領龐大船隊七下西洋的明代艦隊統帥。來源：維基媒體共享資源

1402年，燕王朱棣以「靖難之役」從姪兒建文帝手中奪得帝位，登基為明成祖，惟建文帝於南京城破後下落不明，成為新朝心頭大患——民間甚至傳言他喬裝出走，遠遁海外。永樂三年（1405年），成祖命親信宦官鄭和統率二百餘艘艦船、二萬七千餘人的龐大船隊出使「西洋」，官方說法是宣揚國威、廣招朝貢，但尋訪建文帝下落，亦是朝中公開的揣測之一。鄭和本姓馬，雲南回族，靖難之役中隨侍朱棣立下戰功，深受信任，故被委以此等規模空前的任務。

鄭和艦隊的核心是「寶船」，據《明史》記載最大者長逾四十丈、闊十八丈，桅杆多達九根，是當代世界最龐大的木造帆船，隨行尚有糧船、水船、戰船與馬船等各司其職。船隊編制齊全，除水手兵卒外，另有通曉外語的「通事」、醫官、天文官與工匠隨行，可見遠航講求周全部署而非臨時起意。艦隊每到一地，即以絲綢、瓷器、茶葉與白銀換取當地的香料、象牙、寶石與奇珍異獸；永樂十二年（1414年），使團自東非麻林國帶回長頸鹿，獻於朝廷，被附會為傳說中的祥瑞「麒麟」，轟動一時。

七次遠航前後歷時近三十年，最遠曾抵紅海與東非海岸，締造了當時世界罕見的航海壯舉，但耗費之鉅亦引來朝中儒臣質疑「厚往薄來」勞民傷財。宣德八年（1433年），鄭和於第七次下西洋歸途中病逝於古里（今印度科澤科德），成祖早於1424年駕崩，朝廷內部重文抑武的傾向漸強。成化年間，兵部官員劉大夏等以「舊案無存」為由焚毀或隱匿航海檔案，明廷其後厲行海禁，龐大寶船任其朽壞，中國自此退出了自己曾經領先的遠洋探索舞台。

The Storm That Finished What English Cannon Started

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In summer 1588 Spain, the greatest naval power in Europe, sent some 130 ships to invade England. The invasion failed completely, and Spanish losses were catastrophic. Before reading on, do you think English gunfire won the battle outright — or was something else the bigger killer of ships and men?
Context

Key figures: Philip II of Spain (腓力二世); Elizabeth I of England (伊利沙伯一世); the Duke of Medina Sidonia (梅迪納·西多尼亞公爵), reluctant Spanish fleet commander; Sir Francis Drake (法蘭西斯·德雷克), English vice-admiral.

Event at stake: In 1588 Philip II sent the Spanish Armada (西班牙無敵艦隊) to invade Protestant England and restore Catholic rule.

Big picture: Europe was split by the Reformation into rival Catholic and Protestant powers; Spain was the dominant naval empire of the age, England a rising rival that had been raiding Spanish treasure fleets and had recently executed the Catholic claimant Mary, Queen of Scots.

The Spanish Armada's route around the British Isles in 1588 — the northern retreat, not the Channel battle, caused most of its losses. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Philip II of Spain (腓力二世) had reasons that were religious, dynastic and personal. England's Protestant queen, Elizabeth I (伊利沙伯一世), had for years quietly backed privateers such as Francis Drake (法蘭西斯·德雷克) in raiding Spanish treasure fleets from the Americas, and in 1587 she had executed the Catholic claimant Mary, Queen of Scots. In May 1588 Philip despatched the Spanish Armada (西班牙無敵艦隊): roughly 130 ships and 30,000 men, commanded reluctantly by the Duke of Medina Sidonia (梅迪納·西多尼亞公爵), a nobleman with no naval experience who had begged to be excused. The plan was to sail up the Channel, meet a Spanish army in Flanders, and ferry it across to conquer England.

English ships were smaller but faster and built for long-range gunnery rather than boarding, the traditional Spanish tactic. On 28 July, off Calais (加來), the English sent fireships (火船) drifting into the anchored Armada at night, forcing captains to cut their cables in panic and scatter into open sea. The following day's Battle of Gravelines (格拉沃利訥海戰) saw English guns inflict real damage at range, though it sank few ships outright. More decisive was that the scattered fleet could no longer link up with the army in Flanders, and with the wind turning against a return down the Channel, Medina Sidonia had only one route home left: north, around Scotland and Ireland.

That route proved catastrophic. Unfamiliar with the waters and short of anchors lost at Calais, dozens of Spanish ships were driven onto the rocky coasts of Scotland and Ireland by autumn storms; some estimates put total losses near half the fleet and over half its men, mostly to weather and starvation rather than English cannon. English Protestants later minted a medal reading "God blew and they were scattered," crediting providence over strategy. The defeat did not end the Anglo-Spanish War, which dragged on until 1604, but it punctured the myth of Spanish invincibility at sea and became a founding legend of English national identity.

惠能——不識字的樵夫如何成為震動禪宗的六祖

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
唐代禪宗五祖弘忍座下有數百門人，多年鑽研經教、依次第漸修。他決定以一首偈頌競賽選出傳法弟子，最終勝出的卻是一個不識字、以賣柴為生的嶺南樵夫。你認為，這位樵夫的偈頌講了甚麼，竟勝過寺中修行多年的上座弟子？
背景速覽

核心人物：惠能（638–713年，禪宗六祖）；神秀（禪宗北宗創始者，惠能同門師兄）；五祖弘忍（禪宗五祖，黃梅東山法門）。

核心主張：「頓悟」——見性成佛不假漸修次第；「心即是佛」，本性自足清淨。

時代背景：唐代禪宗五祖弘忍座下數百門人，多數依經教漸次修行；惠能以嶺南樵夫出身，不識字，卻在傳法偈頌競賽中勝出，其後創立南宗頓教，與神秀北宗漸教分庭抗禮。

惠能（638–713年），禪宗六祖，主張頓悟見性、不假漸修次第。來源：維基媒體共享資源

據《六祖壇經》記載，惠能生於嶺南，父早喪，以賣柴奉母，一日聞人誦《金剛經》「應無所住而生其心」，心有所感，遂北上黃梅參謁五祖弘忍。弘忍見他是嶺南「獦獠」，出言試探，惠能答以「人雖有南北，佛性本無南北」，弘忍心奇之，命他到碓房舂米，卻未即傳法。八個月後，弘忍命門人各作一偈以呈心得，作為傳衣缽的依據，上座神秀作偈：「身是菩提樹，心如明鏡台，時時勤拂拭，勿使惹塵埃」，眾人稱善。

惠能聽聞此偈，認為未見本性，遂請人代書一偈：「菩提本無樹，明鏡亦非台，本來無一物，何處惹塵埃」。弘忍見偈，知惠能已悟，卻恐招忌，故意抹去以避人耳目，並於三更密召惠能，為說《金剛經》，至「應無所住而生其心」處惠能大悟，弘忍遂密傳衣缽，命他連夜南逃避禍，因當時法統之爭恐生殺機。惠能隱居獵人隊伍十五年後始於廣州法性寺公開出家傳法，其「頓悟」主張——見性即是成佛，不須依經教漸次修行——與神秀一系的「漸修」形成鮮明對比。

這場分歧的核心，在於「悟」是瞬間直見本性，還是需經年累月拂拭修持才能達致清淨。惠能認為執著於漸修次第，本身即是未見本性、仍住於「有所住」之相；「心即是佛」，眾生本具清淨佛性，只因妄念遮蔽而不自知，一旦言下頓悟，便與佛無異。此說經弟子神會等人大力宣揚，南宗漸成禪宗主流，北宗漸教式微，《六祖壇經》亦成為漢傳佛教中唯一由中國僧人所造、被尊為「經」的著作，其「頓悟」思想其後深刻影響宋明理學與日本禪宗。

The Thought Experiment Nietzsche Called "the Heaviest Weight"

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Imagine a demon tells you that your life, exactly as you have lived it, must repeat itself identically, forever, with nothing new. Nietzsche called this "the greatest weight" and asked whether you would curse it or fall to your knees calling it divine. Before reading on, which reaction do you think he wanted from you — and why would that matter more than the cosmology?
Context

Key figures: Friedrich Nietzsche (尼采, 1844–1900); Arthur Schopenhauer (叔本華), whose pessimism Nietzsche was answering.

Problem at stake: If your life, in every detail, had to repeat itself identically an infinite number of times, would that thought crush you — or transform how you live right now?

Rival view: Schopenhauer held existence itself as suffering best renounced. Nietzsche sought an affirmative answer to the same bleakness without appealing to another world or an afterlife.

Friedrich Nietzsche (尼采), whose thought experiment of eternal recurrence (永劫回歸) asks whether you could will your exact life to repeat forever. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In The Gay Science (1882), Friedrich Nietzsche (尼采) poses a scenario he calls "the greatest weight": a demon appears in your loneliest moment and tells you that your life, exactly as you have lived it, down to every pain and every joy, must be lived again, and again, infinitely, with nothing new. Nietzsche asks: would you curse the demon, or fall to your knees and call it the most divine thing you have ever heard? The question is not primarily about cosmology, though Nietzsche did speculate that a finite universe recurring through infinite time might make it literally true.

The real target is psychological. Nietzsche had grown up steeped in the pessimism of Arthur Schopenhauer (叔本華), for whom existence was suffering best escaped through renunciation. Eternal recurrence (永劫回歸) offered no escape hatch at all — no heaven, no progress, no final redemption, only the same life exactly as lived, forever. Nietzsche's wager was that this apparently crushing thought is actually a test: most people, honestly confronted with it, would recoil in horror at their own lives, revealing how little they have truly affirmed them. The person capable of joyfully willing the repetition is what Nietzsche later called the Übermensch (超人) — not a ruler over others, but one who no longer needs an afterlife to justify existence.

Nietzsche never presents the idea as a metaphysical proof, and scholars still dispute how literally he meant the cosmological claim versus how much it functions purely as an ethical test. The "existential imperative" reading, associated with Bernard Williams and others, treats it strictly as a thought experiment: live such that you could will its exact repetition. This makes eternal recurrence a secular counterpart to Kant's categorical imperative, aimed at your actual life rather than your maxims. Its influence reaches from Milan Kundera's The Unbearable Lightness of Being to contemporary debates about what it would mean to fully affirm one's own life rather than merely endure it.

The Doomsday Cult That Got More Convinced After the World Didn't End

🤔 Predict Before You Read
A small group believed aliens had warned them of a catastrophic flood on a specific date. Members had quit jobs and given away possessions in preparation. Before reading on, what do you think happened to their belief when the date passed and nothing occurred — did they abandon the group, or did something less obvious happen?
Leon Festinger (利昂·費斯廷格), whose 1954 infiltration of a doomsday cult led to the theory of cognitive dissonance (認知失調). Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In 1954, psychologist Leon Festinger (利昂·費斯廷格) read a newspaper item about a small Chicago group, led by a woman he called "Marian Keech" in his published account, who believed she was receiving messages from aliens warning that a catastrophic flood would destroy the world on 21 December. Believers had quit jobs, given away possessions and severed family ties in preparation. Festinger saw a rare opportunity: a group holding a specific, falsifiable, near-future prediction. He and colleagues infiltrated it as observers to see what would happen to belief once the date passed and nothing occurred.

When the appointed hour came and went with no flood and no spaceship, the group did not disband in embarrassment. Within hours, Keech announced a new message: the group's faith had been so powerful it had persuaded the aliens to spare the Earth. Members who had previously avoided publicity began actively proselytising, calling newspapers and seeking converts — the opposite of what a simple "we were wrong" model would predict. Festinger's explanation, developed into the theory of cognitive dissonance (認知失調), was that discovering a costly, public belief was false creates unbearable internal tension, and recruiting others to believe it too is a cheaper way to resolve that tension than admitting the loss.

Cognitive dissonance theory, formalised in Festinger's 1957 book, generalised well beyond doomsday cults: people who have invested effort, money or public commitment in a belief or decision tend to defend it more strongly, not less, when contradicted by new evidence, particularly when abandoning it would be costly or humiliating. Later work applied it to smoking, hazing rituals and post-purchase rationalisation. Critics have noted the original case study lacked a proper control group and relied on Festinger's own interpretive framing of ambiguous events, so the theory's core claim now rests mainly on controlled laboratory work rather than on "When Prophecy Fails" itself, which remains more illustrative than probative.

Why the Same Vegetable Tastes Bitter to You and Bland to Your Friend

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Some people find broccoli, kale and black coffee intensely, unpleasantly bitter; others barely notice any bitterness at all. Before reading on, do you think this is a matter of habit and upbringing, or is there a specific biological difference between people that could explain it?
The tongue's fungiform papillae (蕈狀乳頭), whose density differs between "supertasters" and non-tasters. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In the 1930s, DuPont chemist Arthur Fox (阿瑟·福克斯) accidentally released a cloud of a compound called phenylthiocarbamide (苯硫脲, PTC) in his lab. A colleague nearby complained it tasted intensely bitter; Fox, standing closer, tasted nothing at all. Curious, they tested more people and found the population split cleanly into tasters and non-tasters, a divide that turned out to be genetic and entirely reproducible. The trait became a standard genetics teaching demonstration for decades before anyone understood the underlying mechanism.

The gene responsible, TAS2R38, encodes a bitter-taste receptor on the tongue's taste buds, concentrated in structures called fungiform papillae (蕈狀乳頭). Small variations in this gene change the receptor's shape enough to determine whether it binds strongly to PTC-like compounds — found naturally in cruciferous vegetables like broccoli, kale and Brussels sprouts — or barely binds at all. People with two strong-binding gene copies are "supertasters" (超級味覺者), and don't just taste more bitterness: studies find they also tend to have a higher physical density of fungiform papillae, so they experience more intense sweetness, spiciness and texture generally, not only bitterness.

This is not a story of some people having "better" tastebuds; it is straightforward chemical variation with real dietary consequences. Supertasters, roughly a quarter of the population, often avoid bitter vegetables, black coffee and strong alcohol not from pickiness but because the sensory experience is genuinely more intense and sometimes unpleasant, while non-tasters, roughly another quarter, may need much more seasoning or bitterness to register any flavour at all. Nutrition researchers have linked supertasting to lower vegetable intake in some populations — an unintended consequence of a taste receptor built to warn ancestors away from toxic plant alkaloids, which are often bitter.

💊 Health

The "8 Glasses a Day" Rule Has No Real Origin Study

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Almost everyone has heard that you should drink eight glasses of water a day. Before reading on, where do you think this specific number actually came from — and do you think there's a controlled study behind it?
Clean drinking water — the "8 glasses a day" figure traces to a misquoted 1945 guideline, not a controlled study. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

The advice to drink eight 8-ounce glasses of water daily is one of the most repeated health rules in the English-speaking world, and it has never been traced to a specific scientific study. The closest candidate is a 1945 US Food and Nutrition Board recommendation that adults consume about 2.5 litres of water daily — but that same source noted most of this amount is already contained in food, a qualifying clause that seems to have been dropped somewhere in decades of repetition and simplification into a round, memorable number.

Actual fluid needs vary enormously by body size, activity level, climate and diet, and a large fraction of daily water intake comes from food rather than drinks — fruits, vegetables and soups can supply a substantial share. Heinz Valtin (海因茨·瓦爾丁), a Dartmouth physiologist, reviewed the literature in a widely cited 2002 American Journal of Physiology paper and found no scientific evidence supporting a fixed universal requirement, concluding that healthy adults relying on thirst to guide drinking were adequately hydrated in virtually all studied cases. Thirst, in other words, is a functioning biological signal, not something to override with a quota.

This does not mean hydration is unimportant — the elderly, people in intense heat or exercising heavily, and anyone with certain medical conditions can have blunted thirst signals and genuinely benefit from deliberate fluid tracking. But for the average healthy adult in ordinary conditions, urine colour (pale straw is well-hydrated) is a simpler and more individually accurate guide than a fixed number of glasses. The persistence of the myth may owe less to evidence and more to its clean arithmetic and the interests of a bottled-water industry that benefits from a memorable, universal rule.

🏋 Sports Science

Creatine Doesn't Damage Your Kidneys — Here's Where That Fear Came From

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Creatine is one of the most researched supplements in sports science, yet many people still avoid it for fear it damages the kidneys. Before reading on, do you think decades of research actually support that fear — and if not, where might the myth have come from?
The chemical structure of creatine monohydrate (肌酸), among the most studied and evidence-backed sports supplements. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Creatine monohydrate (肌酸) is among the most researched supplements in sports science, with hundreds of studies since the 1990s consistently showing it improves high-intensity, short-duration performance — strength, sprinting, repeated power output — by increasing the muscle's phosphocreatine stores, which regenerate ATP faster during brief maximal effort. Despite this, a persistent fear that creatine damages the kidneys (腎臟) has followed it for decades, discouraging many otherwise appropriate users.

The fear traces largely to a basic misunderstanding of a routine lab marker. Creatine supplementation raises blood creatinine (肌酸酐), a breakdown product doctors use as a proxy for kidney function, simply because more creatine in the body naturally produces more of its breakdown product. But an elevated creatinine caused by supplementation reflects higher input, not impaired filtration; the kidneys are working normally. Controlled trials directly measuring kidney function, including glomerular filtration rate, in healthy people taking standard doses for periods up to five years have found no evidence of renal harm, including in a well-cited review by Kreider et al. spanning decades of accumulated data.

The genuine caveat is people with pre-existing kidney disease, who should consult a physician before supplementing, since the research base is specifically about healthy kidneys. For everyone else, the International Society of Sports Nutrition's position stand states creatine monohydrate is not only safe at recommended doses but is one of the few supplements with genuinely strong evidence behind its performance claims, alongside caffeine and protein. The kidney scare is a case of a normal, expected lab change being mistaken for organ damage — an error mirrored in the popular belief that high-protein diets similarly harm healthy kidneys.

📈 Investing & Economic Thinking

How Buying Stocks With Borrowed Money Turned a Correction Into a Catastrophe

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In late October 1929 the US stock market fell sharply over a few days, and the decline became one of history's most infamous crashes. Before reading on, why do you think a price decline can spiral out of control so quickly — what mechanism turns falling prices into forced selling?
A crowd gathers outside the New York Stock Exchange (紐約證券交易所) during the 1929 crash, as margin calls forced waves of automatic selling. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

By the late 1920s, buying stocks "on margin" (孖展融資買入) — putting down as little as 10% of a stock's price in cash and borrowing the rest, usually from a broker who had in turn borrowed from a bank — had become common practice on Wall Street. It let ordinary investors control far more stock than their capital alone allowed, and it worked wonderfully as long as prices kept rising: an investor who put down 10% and saw the stock rise 10% had effectively doubled their money. Brokers' loans to margin investors reached roughly $8.5 billion by September 1929, an enormous sum against the era's economy.

The mechanism that made margin dangerous was the margin call (孖展追加保證金通知): if a stock's price fell, the broker demanded more cash to maintain the required equity cushion, and if the investor could not pay, the broker sold the stock immediately to recover the loan. On Black Thursday, 24 October 1929, and especially Black Tuesday, 29 October, prices fell sharply enough to trigger a cascade — falling prices forced margin calls, margin calls forced selling, and forced selling pushed prices down further, triggering the next round. The New York Stock Exchange (紐約證券交易所) saw a record 16 million shares traded on 29 October alone, a volume record that stood for decades.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average (道瓊斯工業平均指數) ultimately fell about 89% from its September 1929 peak to its July 1932 low, a decline the market did not fully recover from, in inflation-adjusted terms, until the 1950s. The crash alone did not cause the Great Depression (大蕭條) that followed — most economic historians point to subsequent banking panics, a collapsing money supply and policy mistakes such as the 1930 Smoot–Hawley tariff as bigger drivers — but the self-reinforcing mechanics of margin selling made the crash itself far steeper and faster than the underlying economic news alone would explain, a lesson regulators cited when instituting margin requirement limits in the 1930s.

⚖️ Political Economics — Theory in the Wild

Why Striking It Rich in Natural Gas Made the Dutch Economy Sicker

Gas processing works at Slochteren, part of the Groningen gas field (格羅寧根天然氣田) whose 1959 discovery gave "Dutch disease" its name. Credit: Wikimedia Commons
🌍 Event
In 1959 the Netherlands discovered the vast Groningen gas field (格羅寧根天然氣田). Gas exports surged through the 1960s–70s, yet manufacturing quietly declined and unemployment rose — a pattern The Economist later named "Dutch disease" (荷蘭病) after this exact case.
📖 Theory
Standard trade theory predicts a resource windfall is unambiguously good: it raises national income and funds investment elsewhere in the economy, with no obvious channel for that wealth to damage other industries.
⚠️ Anomaly
Corden and Neary's 1982 model explained the mechanism: gas exports pushed up the guilder, making manufactured exports pricier and imports cheaper, while the booming sector drew capital and labour from manufacturing — a wealth effect that hollowed out tradeable industry. Sachs and Warner (1995) later found resource-rich countries grew slower on average than resource-poor ones since 1970 across a large cross-country sample.
🧪 Challenger
Institutional economists (Mehlum, Moene & Torvik, 2006) argue the curse isn't automatic: Norway's oil wealth, channelled through a sovereign fund and strong institutions, largely avoided it, while resource wealth harms growth mainly where governance is already weak — suggesting institutional quality, not resources themselves, is the real variable. Open question how much weight each factor deserves.
💡 A resource windfall doesn't guarantee prosperity — it can price your other industries out of existence.
📖 Corden, W.M. & Neary, J.P. (1982), “Booming Sector and De-Industrialisation in a Small Open Economy,” Economic Journal. Sachs, J.D. & Warner, A.M. (1995), NBER Working Paper. Mehlum, H., Moene, K. & Torvik, R. (2006), “Institutions and the Resource Curse,” Economic Journal.

🔗 Connection of the Day

Cards 1 & 11 — Seeing the Shadow, Not the Thing Itself: The Event Horizon Telescope never photographed a black hole; it photographed the shadow gravity casts on the light around it, and had to combine data from telescopes worldwide just to resolve that shadow at all. The Dutch disease debate is a parallel act of inference: nobody can directly observe "institutional quality" harming an economy, only its shadow — a stronger currency, a shrinking factory sector, a resource-rich country growing slower than a resource-poor one. In both cases the object of real interest is invisible, and what looks like a direct photograph or a simple rich-versus-poor comparison is actually a carefully assembled inference from indirect evidence.

So what: When a chart or headline appears to show something directly, ask what indirect signal it's actually a shadow of — the underlying mechanism is usually one inferential step removed from what's displayed.
