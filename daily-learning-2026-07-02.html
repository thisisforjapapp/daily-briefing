🔭 Astronomy & Cosmos

The Comet That Proved Newton Right: How Edmond Halley (哈雷) Predicted a Comet’s Return 16 Years Before It Happened — and Never Lived to See It

🤔 Predict Before You Read
If you saw a bright comet in the sky and someone told you the exact same comet would return in exactly 76 years, decades after your own death, would you believe them? What kind of evidence would convince you?
Halley’s Comet (哈雷彗星) — the first comet recognised as periodic. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In 1705, English astronomer Edmond Halley (哈雷) used Isaac Newton’s (牛頓) newly published laws of motion and gravitation to analyse the orbits of 24 historical comets. He noticed that the comets of 1531, 1607, and 1682 shared strikingly similar orbital paths — and concluded they were not three different comets but one comet returning roughly every 76 years, its orbit slightly perturbed each pass by the gravity of Jupiter (木星) and Saturn (土星). Halley boldly predicted the comet would return in 1758. He died in 1742, sixteen years before the prediction could be tested.

On Christmas night 1758, German amateur astronomer Johann Georg Palitzsch spotted the comet almost exactly on schedule, roughly a month later than the closest calculated approach — an error easily explained by the gravitational tug of the outer planets, which Halley’s contemporaries had not yet been able to model precisely. The comet was named Halley’s Comet (哈雷彗星) in his honour, becoming the first comet recognised as periodic (週期彗星) rather than a one-off omen. The successful prediction was one of the most dramatic public vindications of Newtonian mechanics (牛頓力學) in the 18th century.

Halley’s Comet last appeared in 1986, when the European Space Agency’s Giotto (喬托號) probe flew within 596 kilometres of its nucleus, capturing the first close-up images of a comet’s icy, dark, potato-shaped core — far darker than anyone expected, reflecting only about 4% of incoming sunlight. The comet is due to return in 2061. Ancient records of its passages exist in Chinese, Babylonian, and European chronicles stretching back over two thousand years, including a depiction in the Bayeux Tapestry (貝葉掛毯) commemorating its 1066 appearance before the Norman Conquest.

一場御駕親征化解百年兵禍：宋真宗與寇準如何以《澶淵之盟》換來宋遼百廿年和平

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
假如你是北宋宰相，遼國大軍壓境，皇帝想要南逃遷都，你會如何說服他親自率軍北上，親臨前線鼓舞士氣？
背景速覽

核心人物：宋真宗（北宋第三位皇帝）、寇準（力主親征的宰相）、遼聖宗與蕭太后（遼國統治者）、王欽若（主張南遷的大臣）。

事件：景德元年（1004年）遼軍南侵，同年訂立《澶淵之盟》，宋遼約為兄弟之國，宋每年輸遼歲幣銀十萬兩、絹二十萬匹。

時代背景：北宋建國四十餘年，與北方遼國屢有戰事，宋太宗兩次北伐燕雲十六州均告失敗。遼國承天太后蕭綽與遼聖宗親率二十萬大軍南下，兵鋒直指黃河北岸的澶州，威脅開封。

北宋與遼國形勢圖，澶淵之盟劃定雙方邊界。來源：維基媒體共享資源

景德元年（1004年）閏九月，遼國蕭太后與遼聖宗親率號稱二十萬大軍南侵，連下數城，兵鋒直逼澶州（今河南濮陽），與開封僅一河之隔，朝野震動。大臣王欽若、陳堯叟分別主張遷都金陵或成都，暫避鋒芒。宰相寇準力排眾議，堅持真宗必須御駕親征，並以「陛下若南巡，人心崩潰，敵騎乘勢深入，天下可復保耶」相諫，甚至以辭官相脅，逼真宗下定決心北上澶州前線。

真宗抵達澶州後，登上北城門樓，宋軍將士望見御蓋（皇帝儀仗），士氣為之大振，歡呼聲數十里可聞。與此同時，宋軍以床子弩（大型床弩）射殺遼軍先鋒大將蕭撻凜，遼軍士氣頓挫。遼方見久攻不下，加上補給線過長，遂遣使求和。寇準本欲乘勝追擊，收復燕雲失地，惜真宗畏戰求和心切，最終雙方於澶州訂立盟約：宋遼約為兄弟之國，以白溝河為界，宋方每年輸遼「歲幣」銀十萬兩、絹二十萬匹，史稱《澶淵之盟》。

《澶淵之盟》換來宋遼邊境長達一百二十年的和平，終北宋一朝再無大規模宋遼戰爭，兩國互市貿易繁盛，文化交流頻繁。然而歲幣制度亦開創了以財政換和平的先例，後世史家對此毀譽參半：有謂歲幣支出遠低於用兵成本，實為划算之策；亦有謂此舉助長遼國國力，並埋下北宋「守內虛外」重文輕武積弱之因。寇準本人因促成親征立下大功，卻旋即遭王欽若構陷罷相，君臣情誼終究敵不過朝廷權鬥。

The Last Great Galley Battle: How the Holy League (神聖同盟) Shattered Ottoman Naval Power at Lepanto — and Nearly Killed a Young Miguel de Cervantes (塞萬提斯)

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In 1571, over 400 ships and 150,000 men fought in a single day using tactics essentially unchanged since ancient Rome — oar-powered galleys ramming and boarding each other. What would make this the last major battle of its kind, rather than the first of a new era?
Context

Key figures: Don John of Austria (唐胡安), illegitimate half-brother of Philip II of Spain, commander of the Holy League fleet; Ali Pasha (阿里帕夏), Ottoman fleet commander, killed in battle; Pope Pius V (庇護五世), who organised the Holy League coalition.

Event at stake: Battle of Lepanto (勒班陀海戰), 7 October 1571, Gulf of Patras (帕特雷灣), off western Greece.

Big picture: The Ottoman Empire (鄂圖曼帝國) had dominated the eastern Mediterranean for decades, capturing Cyprus (塞浦路斯) from Venice earlier in 1571. A Christian naval defeat risked opening the western Mediterranean to Ottoman expansion.

The Battle of Lepanto (勒班陀海戰), 1571, painted by Laureys a Castro. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

By 1571, the Ottoman Empire (鄂圖曼帝國) controlled the eastern Mediterranean and had just seized Cyprus (塞浦路斯) from Venice (威尼斯), executing its Venetian governor by flaying him alive. Pope Pius V (庇護五世) assembled the Holy League (神聖同盟) — Spain, Venice, the Papal States, and smaller Italian states — pooling over 200 galleys under the young commander Don John of Austria (唐胡安), King Philip II’s illegitimate half-brother, then only 24 years old. Facing them at the Gulf of Patras were roughly 230 Ottoman galleys under Ali Pasha (阿里帕夏), confident after decades of Ottoman naval dominance.

The battle on 7 October 1571 was fought largely by galleys (槳帆船) — oar-powered vessels essentially unchanged since antiquity, closing to ram and board one another. The Holy League’s decisive innovation was six Venetian galleasses (加雷賽戰船), heavier hybrid ships bristling with cannon, positioned ahead of the main line to break up the Ottoman formation before contact. Roughly six hours of brutal close-quarters fighting followed. Ali Pasha was killed and beheaded, his head reportedly displayed on a pike to devastate Ottoman morale. By day’s end, the League had destroyed or captured over 200 Ottoman ships and freed roughly 12,000 Christian galley slaves, at a cost of around 7,500 League dead — among them a 24-year-old Spanish soldier named Miguel de Cervantes (塞萬提斯), who took two gunshot wounds and permanently lost use of his left hand.

Lepanto (勒班陀海戰) was celebrated across Christian Europe as a providential victory halting Ottoman expansion, and it did end Ottoman naval dominance in the western Mediterranean. But the strategic impact proved more limited than the propaganda suggested: the Ottomans rebuilt their fleet within a year, and Venice ultimately ceded Cyprus permanently in the 1573 peace settlement. Lepanto is remembered less for its strategic consequences than as a symbolic hinge point — the last major battle fought primarily by oar-powered galleys before cannon-armed sailing ships came to dominate naval warfare within a generation.

「越名教而任自然」：阮籍如何以醉酒佯狂周旋於司馬氏權力鬥爭之間，活出魏晉風度的極限

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
假如你活在一個講真話會招致滅族之禍的時代，你會選擇沉默、逢迎，還是像阮籍一樣，終日買醉、口不臧否人物，用瘋癲掩護內心的清醒？
背景速覽

核心人物：阮籍（210–263年）、嵇康（竹林七賢之一，後被司馬氏處死）、山濤、劉伶、向秀等竹林七賢；司馬昭（實際掌權者，覬覦帝位）。

核心思想：「越名教而任自然」（超越禮教束縛，順應自然本性）；「口不臧否人物」（不評論他人是非以自保）；《大人先生傳》諷刺禮法之士如「褌中之蝨」。

時代背景：三國曹魏末年，司馬氏集團通過高平陵之變（249年）逐步奪權，朝中名士動輒因言獲罪、慘遭誅殺（嵇康即因得罪鍾會而被處死）。竹林七賢聚於山陽（今河南修武）竹林之下，飲酒、清談、彈琴，以放浪形骸對抗高壓政治。

司馬昭畫像——阮籍終其一生周旋迴避的實際掌權者。來源：維基媒體共享資源

正始十年（249年），司馬懿發動高平陵之變，誅殺曹爽及其黨羽，曹魏政權實權自此落入司馬氏之手。此後十餘年間，凡與司馬氏立場不合、或僅是言論稍有不慎的名士，動輒滅族。阮籍身處這樣的高壓時代，卻拒絕明確表態擁護或反對司馬氏，他選擇了一條極為特殊的自保之道：終日縱酒、行為狂放、口不臧否人物——即從不公開評論任何人物的是非對錯，即使司馬昭多次試探性地向他求親、求官，他也總能以醉酒為由巧妙迴避，長醉六十日以拒絕與司馬昭聯姻的著名典故，正是他政治智慧的體現。

阮籍與嵇康、山濤、劉伶、向秀、王戎、阮咸七人常聚於山陽（今河南修武）竹林之下，縱酒清談、彈琴長嘯，後世稱為「竹林七賢」。阮籍在其《大人先生傳》中，以極盡諷刺之筆調，將恪守禮法的士人比喻為「群蝨之處褌中」——躲在褲襠裡的蝨子，自以為安全，卻不知褲子隨時可能被燒毀，暗諷那些依附司馬氏禮法招牌謀求富貴的士大夫，終將與所依附的政權一同覆滅。他提出「越名教而任自然」的主張，認為儒家禮教（名教）已淪為權力鬥爭的工具，唯有回歸人性本然，才是真正的道德。

阮籍的自保策略最終保全了性命，但他的摯友嵇康卻選擇了截然不同的道路——公開拒絕與司馬氏合作，並在〈與山巨源絕交書〉中直斥「循名教而非自然」，最終因得罪權臣鍾會，於263年被司馬昭處死於刑場，臨刑前索琴彈奏一曲〈廣陵散〉，從容赴死。阮籍與嵇康一狂放避世、一剛烈抗爭，代表了魏晉名士面對政治高壓的兩種極端應對方式，二者共同定義了後世所稱「魏晉風度」——在禮教崩壞、生死無常的時代，以個體真性情對抗虛偽建制的精神姿態，深刻影響了後世文人（如陶淵明、李白）的處世哲學。

Why “It Explains Everything” Is a Red Flag: Karl Popper’s (卡爾·波普爾) Falsificationism (可證偽性) and the Line Between Science and Pseudoscience

🤔 Predict Before You Read
A theory that can explain literally any possible observation — no matter what happens, it always fits — sounds impressively powerful. Why might a philosopher of science consider that a fatal weakness rather than a strength?
Context

Key figures: Karl Popper (卡爾·波普爾, 1902–1994), Vienna-born philosopher of science; the Vienna Circle (維也納學派), logical positivists Popper argued against; Albert Einstein (愛因斯坦), whose 1919 eclipse-tested predictions Popper held up as the model of good science.

Core ideas: Falsificationism (可證偽性) — a theory is scientific only if it can, in principle, be proven false; the demarcation problem (劃界問題) — distinguishing science from pseudoscience; asymmetry between verification and falsification.

Big picture: Early 20th-century philosophy of science, dominated by logical positivism, sought to define scientific knowledge through verification. Popper, troubled by Marxism (馬克思主義) and Freudian psychoanalysis (弗洛伊德精神分析) claiming scientific status while explaining every possible outcome, proposed a radically different criterion.

Karl Popper (卡爾·波普爾), whose falsificationism reshaped the philosophy of science. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In the 1920s, Karl Popper (卡爾·波普爾) noticed something troubling while comparing Einstein’s (愛因斯坦) general relativity to Marxist theory of history and Freudian psychoanalysis (弗洛伊德精神分析). Einstein’s theory made a precise, risky prediction — that light from distant stars would bend by a specific measurable amount during a solar eclipse — which could have failed the 1919 Eddington expedition test and disproven the theory outright. Marxism and psychoanalysis, by contrast, seemed to explain every possible human behaviour after the fact, no matter what happened. Popper concluded the difference wasn’t the amount of confirming evidence each theory could muster, but whether the theory forbade anything — whether it could, even in principle, be proven wrong.

This became falsificationism (可證偽性): a theory only counts as scientific if it makes predictions specific enough that some conceivable observation could refute it. Popper inverted the traditional logic of science. Where the Vienna Circle’s (維也納學派) logical positivists sought verification — piling up confirming instances to prove a theory true — Popper argued no finite number of confirmations can ever prove a general theory true, since a single counterexample can always refute it later. Science therefore progresses not by accumulating proof but through bold conjectures (大膽推測) followed by rigorous attempts at refutation (反駁) — theories that survive repeated, serious attempts to falsify them are provisionally retained, never proven with certainty.

Popper’s demarcation problem (劃界問題) reshaped how working scientists and philosophers think about theory-building: a hypothesis’s virtue lies in what it rules out, not what it can accommodate. Critics, including Thomas Kuhn (孔恩) and Imre Lakatos (拉卡托斯), later argued real scientific practice is messier — theories are rarely abandoned after a single falsifying observation, and auxiliary hypotheses often absorb apparent refutations. Yet falsificationism remains the default intuitive standard many scientists invoke informally: astrology, homeopathy, and unfalsifiable conspiracy theories are still commonly dismissed using precisely Popper’s original test — does this claim forbid anything from happening?

Why Attractive People Get Better Performance Reviews: The Halo Effect (光環效應) and How One Trait Silently Colours Everything Else We Judge

🤔 Predict Before You Read
You’re rating a stranger’s intelligence, kindness, and honesty based only on a photograph of their face. Do you think their physical attractiveness would influence your ratings of traits that have nothing to do with appearance — and if so, how much?
Edward Thorndike (桑代克), who first documented the halo effect in 1920. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In 1920, psychologist Edward Thorndike (桑代克) asked military officers to rate their soldiers on unrelated traits — physical qualities, intelligence, leadership, character. He found the ratings were far more correlated than they should have been: officers who rated a soldier highly on one positive trait tended to rate him highly across all traits, even ones they had no real basis for judging. Thorndike called this the halo effect (光環效應): a single global impression — often triggered by something as superficial as physical appearance or a first strong impression — contaminates judgements of specific, unrelated qualities.

The effect has been replicated extensively far beyond the military. Landy & Sigall (1974) found identical essays received significantly higher quality ratings when attributed to an attractive-photographed author than an unattractive one. Nisbett & Wilson (1977) showed that students who watched a warm, likeable lecturer on video rated his physical appearance, mannerisms, and even his accent more favourably than students who watched the same lecturer being cold and distant — despite both groups viewing the identical person, differing only in one dimension of warmth. Participants had no conscious awareness that the warmth manipulation was driving their other judgements at all.

The halo effect has measurable real-world consequences: attractive job candidates receive more favourable competence ratings and higher starting salary offers in controlled studies; attractive defendants receive systematically lighter sentences in mock-jury research; and companies with likeable, charismatic CEOs are often rated by investors as having stronger fundamentals even when financial performance is held constant — a bias business writer Phil Rosenzweig termed “the halo effect” in corporate strategy research, showing that retrospective narratives about “great companies” often just describe companies that happened to perform well, with every trait reinterpreted favourably in hindsight.

🫀 Physiology

180 Litres a Day, Only 1.5 Come Out: How the Nephron (腎元) Filters Your Entire Blood Supply 40 Times Daily and Decides Exactly What to Keep

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Your kidneys filter your entire blood plasma volume roughly 60 times per day, producing about 180 litres of filtrate — yet you only urinate about 1.5 litres. Where does the other 178.5 litres go?
The nephron (腎元) — the kidney’s microscopic filtration unit. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Each kidney contains roughly one million nephrons (腎元), the microscopic functional units where blood filtration happens. Blood enters each nephron’s glomerulus (腎小球) — a tangled ball of capillaries — under enough pressure to force water, salts, glucose, and small molecules through a filtering membrane, while holding back blood cells and large proteins. This process, glomerular filtration (腎小球過濾), produces roughly 180 litres of filtrate per day across both kidneys — about 60 times your entire blood plasma volume — yet a healthy adult urinates only around 1.5 litres daily. The other 178.5 litres are almost entirely reclaimed.

That reclamation happens along the renal tubule (腎小管), a long, winding structure the filtrate travels through after leaving the glomerulus. In the proximal tubule (近曲小管), roughly 65% of filtered water, virtually all glucose, and most salts are actively reabsorbed back into the bloodstream. The loop of Henle (亨利氏環) creates a concentration gradient deep in the kidney’s medulla, enabling final urine to be concentrated far beyond blood plasma when needed. The distal tubule and collecting duct (集合管) make the final fine adjustments, governed by hormones — aldosterone (醛固酮) controls sodium reabsorption, and antidiuretic hormone (抗利尿激素, ADH) controls how much water is pulled back out.

This system explains familiar everyday experiences: drink a large glass of water and ADH secretion drops, less water is reabsorbed, and dilute urine follows within roughly 30–45 minutes; become dehydrated and ADH rises sharply, concentrating urine to a dark yellow as the kidneys claw back every reclaimable drop. Kidney filtration rate — measured clinically as glomerular filtration rate (腎小球過濾率, GFR) — is the single most important marker of kidney function, and it naturally declines with age, which is why dosing of many medications must be adjusted for older adults even without diagnosed kidney disease.

Check your answer
The renal tubules reabsorb almost all of the filtered water and useful solutes back into the bloodstream — about 65% in the proximal tubule alone — with hormones like ADH and aldosterone fine-tuning the final amount, so only a small fraction of the original filtrate becomes urine.

How Much Salt Is Actually Dangerous? The DASH-Sodium Trial (得舒飲食鈉研究), Salt Sensitivity, and Why the Guidelines Are More Contested Than You’d Think

🤔 Predict Before You Read
The World Health Organization recommends under 5 grams of salt per day. Most people in developed countries eat roughly 9–12 grams. Do you think cutting everyone’s intake to the recommended level would lower blood pressure by roughly the same amount for everyone — or does it vary a lot person to person?
Blood pressure measurement — sodium intake is one of several modifiable drivers of hypertension (高血壓). Credit: Wikimedia Commons

High sodium intake (鈉攝取量) has been linked to hypertension (高血壓) since the landmark INTERSALT study (1988), which compared salt intake and blood pressure across 52 population groups worldwide and found populations with very low sodium diets had essentially no age-related blood pressure rise, while high-sodium populations showed the typical increase. The DASH-Sodium trial (Sacks et al., 2001, NEJM) provided the strongest controlled evidence: participants on the DASH diet combined with reduced sodium showed the largest blood pressure reductions, with effects strongest in people already diagnosed with hypertension and in Black participants specifically.

What complicates the simple “salt is bad” message is salt sensitivity (鹽敏感性) — individual variation in how strongly blood pressure responds to sodium intake. Roughly 25–50% of people with hypertension are classified as salt-sensitive, while many normotensive people show minimal response even to substantial sodium changes. Genetic factors, kidney sodium-handling efficiency, age, and the sodium-to-potassium ratio (鈉鉀比例) in the overall diet all modulate the relationship — a diet high in potassium-rich foods appears to blunt sodium’s blood-pressure-raising effect independent of sodium reduction itself.

The practical picture: for the general population, reducing sodium modestly produces small-to-moderate blood pressure benefits at the population level with minimal downside, which is why WHO and most national guidelines recommend it. But aggressive individual sodium restriction below roughly 3g/day has shown mixed results in some cohort studies, including a controversial 2011 JAMA meta-analysis suggesting a possible J-shaped risk curve at very low intakes — disputed by many epidemiologists as confounded by reverse causation. For most people, the highest-value intervention remains eating less ultra-processed food, which supplies roughly 70% of dietary sodium in most Western diets, rather than obsessively avoiding the salt shaker.

🏋 Sports Science

Why Elite Athletes Deliberately Train Less: Deload Weeks (減量週), Overtraining Syndrome (過度訓練綜合症), and the Science of Planned Recovery

🤔 Predict Before You Read
If more training volume generally produces more strength and muscle gains, why would a coach deliberately schedule a week of dramatically reduced training every few weeks — seemingly working against the very progress they’re trying to build?
Structured training programmes rely on planned recovery, not just accumulating volume. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Training adaptation doesn’t happen during exercise itself — it happens during recovery, when the body repairs and supercompensates (超量恢復) beyond its previous baseline in response to the stress just imposed. Consistently training hard without adequate recovery creates accumulating fatigue that eventually outpaces the body’s ability to adapt, a state sports scientists call functional overreaching (功能性過度訓練) when short-term and reversible, or overtraining syndrome (過度訓練綜合症, OTS) when it becomes a persistent, weeks-to-months-long performance decline accompanied by hormonal disruption and elevated injury risk. The European College of Sport Science’s 2013 consensus statement (Meeusen et al.) defines OTS partly by its resistance to short recovery — performance stays depressed even after a few days of rest.

A deload week (減量週) — typically reducing training volume by 40–60% while maintaining or slightly reducing intensity — is a planned intervention designed to dissipate accumulated fatigue before it crosses into overreaching territory, allowing the nervous system and connective tissues to fully recover while largely preserving fitness adaptations, which decay far more slowly than fatigue accumulates. Unlike complete rest, a well-designed deload keeps movement patterns active while removing the mechanical and systemic stress load that was driving the fatigue in the first place.

How often a deload is needed varies enormously by training experience, intensity, and individual recovery capacity — novice lifters training moderately may go 8–12 weeks between deloads, while advanced athletes training near their physiological ceiling may need one every 3–5 weeks. Autoregulated approaches, using tools like heart rate variability (心率變異度, HRV) or subjective readiness questionnaires, increasingly replace rigid fixed schedules, triggering a deload based on measured fatigue markers rather than a predetermined calendar. The core principle remains the same: fitness gains are the sum of stress and recovery together, and skipping the recovery half eventually caps or reverses progress entirely.

📈 Investing & Economic Thinking

The PhD Thesis That Made Diversification a Science: Harry Markowitz (馬科維茲) and Modern Portfolio Theory (現代投資組合理論)

🤔 Predict Before You Read
If two individual stocks are each fairly risky on their own, is it possible to combine them into a portfolio that is meaningfully less risky than either stock alone — without sacrificing expected return? What would have to be true about how the two stocks move relative to each other?
Modern Portfolio Theory underpins how diversified portfolios are constructed across public markets today. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In 1952, 25-year-old University of Chicago doctoral student Harry Markowitz (馬科維茲) published a 14-page paper in the Journal of Finance that transformed how the investment industry thinks about risk. Before Markowitz, the standard approach evaluated each asset in isolation, judged on its own expected return and risk. Markowitz’s insight, later called Modern Portfolio Theory (現代投資組合理論, MPT), was that what matters is not any single asset’s risk but how assets move together — their covariance (共變異數). Combining assets that don’t move in lockstep can reduce a portfolio’s overall volatility below what any individual holding would suggest, without necessarily reducing expected return.

Markowitz formalised this into the efficient frontier (效率前緣): for any target level of expected return, there exists a specific combination of assets that minimises risk, and portfolios below this frontier are needlessly risky for their return. The mathematics required calculating the correlation between every pair of assets in a portfolio — computationally prohibitive by hand in 1952, one reason MPT’s practical adoption lagged decades behind its publication until computing power caught up. Markowitz shared the 1990 Nobel Prize in Economics for this work, alongside William Sharpe, who built the Capital Asset Pricing Model (資本資產定價模型, CAPM) directly on Markowitz’s foundation.

MPT’s core assumptions have drawn substantial criticism: it assumes returns are normally distributed (常態分佈) and that historical correlations reliably predict future correlations — both assumptions that break down precisely during crises, when previously uncorrelated assets often move together as investors sell everything simultaneously, as in 2008 and briefly in 2020. Despite these limitations, MPT’s central insight — that diversification (分散投資) across imperfectly correlated assets genuinely reduces risk without proportionally sacrificing return — remains the theoretical bedrock underlying target-date retirement funds, robo-advisors, and the entire modern asset-allocation industry.

⚖️ Political Economics — Theory in the Wild

Can a Fixed Currency Peg Buy Credibility Forever? Argentina’s Convertibility Plan (可兌換計劃) and Its 2001 Collapse

Argentina’s 1991–2001 currency board pegged the peso 1:1 to the US dollar. Credit: Wikimedia Commons
🌍 Event
In 1991, Argentina’s economy minister Domingo Cavallo (卡瓦略) launched the Convertibility Plan (可兌換計劃), pegging the peso (阿根廷比索) 1:1 to the US dollar via a currency board (貨幣發行局). It ended hyperinflation (惡性通脹) that had exceeded 3,000% annually. By December 2001, the same policy triggered the corralito banking freeze and a sovereign default (主權違約) — then the largest in history — alongside 25% unemployment and riots that toppled the government within two weeks.
📖 Theory
The currency board’s designers argued a hard, legally binding peg would import monetary credibility (貨幣信譽) Argentina’s central bank could never achieve alone after decades of inflationary financing, permanently anchoring inflation expectations by removing the government’s ability to print money to cover deficits.
⚠️ Anomaly
The peg worked for inflation but created a rigid straitjacket: when Brazil devalued in 1999 and the US dollar strengthened globally, Argentine exports became sharply uncompetitive, but the currency board offered no exchange-rate valve to absorb the shock. GDP fell nearly 28% over four years (1998–2002) as the economy could only adjust through brutal wage and price deflation rather than devaluation.
🧪 Challenger
Mundell’s optimal currency area (最適貨幣區) theory predicts exactly this outcome: fixed rates work only between economies facing correlated shocks — a condition Argentina and the US never met. Critics argue the deeper failure was fiscal, not monetary: provinces kept borrowing as if the currency board didn’t constrain them. Open question: whether any fixed-rate regime can survive sustained fiscal indiscipline, regardless of its monetary credibility design.
💡 A currency peg can buy credibility against inflation, but it borrows against future flexibility — when a real shock arrives, someone still has to absorb it, one way or another.
📖 Cavallo, D. & Cottani, J. (1997). “Argentina’s Convertibility Plan.” American Economic Review Papers. Mussa, M. (2002). Argentina and the Fund. IIE. IMF Independent Evaluation Office (2004). “The IMF and Argentina, 1991–2001.”

🔗 Connection of the Day

Cards 5 & 11 — When a Framework Meets Its Falsifying Case: Karl Popper argued a theory earns scientific credibility precisely by making risky, falsifiable predictions. Argentina’s Convertibility Plan was, in effect, a real-world economic experiment: the theory that a hard currency peg imports permanent monetary credibility made a specific, falsifiable prediction — that the peg could survive external shocks. The 1999–2002 collapse was the falsifying observation, exposing exactly where the theory's assumptions broke down.

So what: Treat confident economic and policy frameworks the way Popper treated scientific theories — ask what observation would prove them wrong, and pay closest attention when that observation actually arrives, rather than explaining it away after the fact.
