The Week Humanity Watched a Comet Punch Earth-Sized Holes in Jupiter

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In July 1994, astronomers around the world pointed every available telescope at Jupiter and waited. A comet had been torn into a line of fragments and was about to hit the planet, the first collision between two solar system bodies humans would ever watch happen. Before reading on, what do you think the impacts actually looked like through a telescope, and why did the sight change how scientists thought about Earth’s own safety?
Dark impact scars strung across Jupiter’s (木星) southern hemisphere in July 1994, photographed by the Hubble Space Telescope (哈勃太空望遠鏡). Some were larger than Earth. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In March 1993, Carolyn and Eugene Shoemaker (蘇梅克夫婦) and David Levy (列維) found something odd on a photographic plate at Palomar Observatory (帕洛瑪天文台): a comet that appeared smeared into a line, which press coverage soon nicknamed the “string of pearls.” It was not one object but roughly twenty fragments strung across the sky. Calculations showed the comet had passed too close to Jupiter (木星) in 1992 and been pulled apart by tidal forces, and that it was already captured in orbit around the planet. Then came the extraordinary part: the orbit ended at Jupiter itself, in July 1994.

For the first time, humanity had roughly sixteen months’ notice of a collision between two solar system bodies. Nearly every major telescope was retasked, including the newly repaired Hubble Space Telescope (哈勃太空望遠鏡) and the Galileo spacecraft (伽利略號), which happened to be en route to Jupiter and had a direct view of the impact side. Between 16 and 22 July 1994, fragment after fragment slammed into Jupiter’s southern hemisphere at around 60 kilometres per second. Many astronomers had publicly predicted the impacts would be barely visible, gas striking gas with nothing solid to mark.

They were wrong, spectacularly. Each impact threw up a fireball of superheated material that rose thousands of kilometres above the cloud tops, then fell back to leave a dark bruise of sooty debris in the atmosphere. Some scars were larger than Earth and visible in modest backyard telescopes for months. The event did more than provide pictures: it made planetary impact an observed phenomenon rather than a theoretical one, and it strengthened the case that Jupiter’s gravity intercepts a meaningful share of inbound objects. Within a few years, funding for systematic near-Earth object surveys had expanded substantially.

阿育王——一場屠城之後，一位征服者為何在岩石上刻下懺悔

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
公元前三世紀，印度孔雀王朝一位君主為了統一全境，發動了一場死傷極其慘重的征服戰爭。你認為他勝利之後會如何？歷史上絕大多數征服者都會刻碑誇耀武功，而他卻做了一件當時前所未見的事——你猜是甚麼？
背景速覽

核心人物：阿育王（Ashoka，約公元前304–232年，孔雀王朝第三代君主）；其祖父旃陀羅笈多為王朝開創者。

事件：約公元前261年羯陵伽（Kalinga，今印度奧里薩邦一帶）征服戰役，及其後阿育王在全境岩壁與石柱上頒刻的詔敕。

時代背景：孔雀王朝是印度史上首個統一大部分次大陸的帝國。當時婆羅門祭祀傳統仍佔主導，而佛教與耆那教等沙門思潮正在興起，挑戰種姓與祭祀權威。阿育王的詔敕成為印度最早可靠斷代的成文史料之一。

桑吉大塔（Sanchi）南門浮雕，描繪阿育王前往佛塔禮拜的場面。來源：維基媒體共享資源

孔雀王朝由旃陀羅笈多在公元前四世紀末創立，趁亞歷山大東征後印度西北權力真空之際崛起。傳至第三代阿育王時，帝國已涵蓋今日印度、巴基斯坦與阿富汗大部分地區，唯獨東岸的羯陵伽尚未臣服。約公元前261年，阿育王發兵征服羯陵伽。按他自己後來頒布的詔敕記載，此役有十萬人被殺，十五萬人被擄，另有數倍於此的人因戰亂饑疫而死。數字或有誇大，但同一份文獻由勝利者親自刻下，且用以自責而非誇耀，這在古代世界極為罕見。

戰後阿育王的轉向，最可靠的證據不是後世佛教傳說，而是他自己下令刻在岩壁與石柱上的三十餘篇詔敕，散布於帝國各地，以當地通行的俗語（普拉克里特語）書寫，並在西北邊區另以希臘語及阿拉美語並刻，顯然是要讓百姓而非官僚讀懂。第十三號摩崖詔敕直言，他對羯陵伽死傷「深感悔恨與悲痛」，並宣布此後改以「法」（Dhamma）而非武力治國。所謂「法」並非單指佛教教義，而是一套跨宗派的公共倫理：孝順父母、善待僕役、不殺生、對各宗派保持寬容。

詔敕中亦記載具體措施：在道路旁植樹掘井、設立人與牲畜的醫療設施、派遣「法大官」巡察民情、禁止若干祭祀性動物屠宰，並宣稱王室廚房的肉食已大幅削減。他同時派遣使節遠赴斯里蘭卡、希臘化諸王國宣揚佛法，這是佛教走出印度的關鍵一步。阿育王死後不足半世紀，孔雀王朝即告瓦解，其詔敕所用的婆羅米文亦逐漸失傳，直至1837年英國學者普林塞普（James Prinsep）成功解讀，這位君主才重新為世人所知。今日印度國徽上的四獅柱頭，正取自他所立的鹿野苑石柱。

The Volcano That Destroyed a Roman Town by Preserving It

🤔 Predict Before You Read
When Vesuvius erupted in AD 79, the Roman town of Pompeii was buried. Nearly two millennia later, archaeologists could read graffiti off its walls, identify what was on the stove, and see the shapes of individual people at the moment they died. Before reading on, what do you think about this particular eruption made that possible, when most disasters destroy the evidence of themselves?
Context

Key figures: Pliny the Elder (老普林尼), naturalist and fleet commander who died in the eruption; Pliny the Younger (小普林尼), his nephew, whose letters are the only surviving eyewitness account; Giuseppe Fiorelli (菲奧雷利), the nineteenth-century excavator who invented the plaster-cast technique.

Event at stake: The eruption of Mount Vesuvius (維蘇威火山) in AD 79, which buried Pompeii (龐貝) and Herculaneum (赫庫蘭尼姆).

Big picture: Pompeii was an ordinary, prosperous Campanian town of perhaps 11,000 people, not a capital or a monument. That ordinariness is exactly why it matters: almost everything else we know about Roman daily life comes from elite writing, while Pompeii preserved the bakery, the brothel, the election slogans and the shopping lists.

The excavated street grid of Pompeii (龐貝), with Mount Vesuvius (維蘇威火山) behind. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

The eruption came in two very different phases, and the difference is why Pompeii survives. First, a column of ash and pumice rose more than thirty kilometres into the sky and drifted south-east, raining light pumice over Pompeii for many hours. This buried the town gradually and collapsed many roofs under the weight, but it did not sweep it away, and it gave most inhabitants time to flee. Then, overnight, the column collapsed and sent pyroclastic surges — avalanches of gas and ash moving at hundreds of kilometres per hour and hot enough to kill instantly — down the mountain.

Pliny the Younger (小普林尼), then seventeen, watched from Misenum across the bay and described it decades later in two letters to the historian Tacitus (塔西佗). He compared the eruption column to an umbrella pine, a description so accurate that volcanologists still call this eruption type “Plinian.” His uncle, Pliny the Elder (老普林尼), commanded the fleet and sailed toward the disaster both to observe it and to evacuate people; he came ashore at Stabiae and died there, probably from the effects of the gas. Modern dating work has cast doubt on the traditional 24 August date, with evidence including a charcoal inscription pointing to mid-October.

What makes Pompeii unique is that the ash sealed the town in an oxygen-poor envelope, halting the decay that normally erases organic material and everyday clutter. Bread was found still in the oven, and painted election notices survive on street walls. Bodies decayed inside the hardened ash, leaving voids; in 1863 Giuseppe Fiorelli (菲奧雷利) realised these voids could be filled with plaster to recover the exact posture of a person at the moment of death. The result is uncomfortable and irreplaceable: the single richest record we have of how ordinary Romans actually lived.

道元「只管打坐」——為何他說坐禪不是開悟的手段，而就是開悟本身

🤔 閱讀前先猜猜
一位日本僧人年少時心中有一大疑：既然佛經說眾生「本來是佛」，本自具足佛性，那麼歷代祖師為何還要辛苦修行？他為此遠渡宋朝求法。你認為他最後得到的答案，是修行有其必要，還是修行根本多餘？抑或問題本身就問錯了？
背景速覽

核心人物：道元（1200–1253年），日本曹洞宗開祖；其在宋朝的師父為天童山如淨禪師。

核心思想：「只管打坐」（純粹地坐，不為求任何結果）與「修證一等」（修行與證悟並非手段與目的，而是同一件事）。

時代背景：鎌倉時代初期，日本佛教由平安朝的天台、真言貴族佛教，轉向面向武士與庶民的新宗派。同期有法然、親鸞的淨土念佛與榮西傳入的臨濟禪。道元二十四歲入宋，正值中國禪宗盛行公案參究之時。

道元禪師（1200–1253）畫像——日本曹洞宗開祖，主張「只管打坐」「修證一等」。來源：維基媒體共享資源

道元出身京都貴族，幼年喪母，十三歲上比叡山出家。據其傳記所載，他在天台教學中遇上一個難以化解的疑問：既然《大般涅槃經》等經典明言一切眾生「本來本法性，天然自性身」，本自是佛，何以三世諸佛還要發心修行？這個疑問在比叡山未獲解答，他遂轉投建仁寺榮西門下，其後於貞應二年（1223年）隨師兄明全入宋求法。

在宋期間，道元遍參諸山而未愜於心，直至在天童山遇如淨禪師。如淨主張坐禪即是「身心脫落」，反對當時流行的以參公案求開悟、把坐禪當作工具的風氣。道元由此徹悟。他所得的答案不是「要修」或「不必修」，而是問題預設了錯誤的框架：把修行看成手段、把證悟看成將來要取得的成果，本身就是一種執取。若佛性本自具足，則坐禪不是為了將來成佛，坐禪本身就是佛的行為在當下展現。他稱之為「修證一等」。

回國後，道元著《普勸坐禪儀》與畢生大作《正法眼藏》，力倡「只管打坐」：只是坐，不求開悟，不求安寧，不求任何回報。他在〈現成公案〉篇中留下著名一段：「學佛道者，學自己也；學自己者，忘自己也；忘自己者，為萬法所證也。」為避開京都舊教團的壓力，他晚年遠赴越前山中創建永平寺，拒絕接近權貴。曹洞宗此後在日本地方社會扎根，而《正法眼藏》艱深的思辨性文字，到二十世紀才被和辻哲郎等學者重新發掘，成為日本哲學研究的核心文本之一。

The Thought Experiment About a Runaway Tram That Was Never About Trams

🤔 Predict Before You Read
A runaway tram will kill five people. You can divert it onto a side track where it will kill one. Most people say divert. Now: the tram will kill five, and you can stop it only by pushing one large stranger off a bridge into its path. Almost nobody says push. Before reading on, why do you think the numbers being identical does not settle the question — and what is the difference actually tracking?
Context

Key figures: Philippa Foot (菲莉帕·富特, 1920–2010), British moral philosopher; Judith Jarvis Thomson (湯姆森, 1929–2020), who devised the footbridge variant and named the problem.

Problem at stake: Whether the moral difference between doing harm and merely allowing it, and between intending harm and foreseeing it, can be defended — or whether only outcomes matter.

Rival view: Foot was writing against both crude utilitarianism, which counts only totals, and an unexamined version of the Doctrine of Double Effect inherited from Catholic moral theology. Her 1967 paper was about abortion, not transport.

The trolley problem (電車難題): divert the tram to kill one instead of five? Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Philippa Foot (菲莉帕·富特) introduced the runaway tram in a 1967 essay whose actual subject was abortion and the Doctrine of Double Effect (雙重效果原則), the principle that it can be permissible to cause a harm you foresee but do not intend, as a side effect of pursuing a good end. Foot suspected that principle was doing less work than its defenders thought. Her tram case was designed as a control: here is a situation where diverting seems clearly permissible, so what exactly distinguishes it from the cases where killing one to save five seems monstrous? She proposed that the real distinction lies between doing and allowing harm.

Judith Jarvis Thomson (湯姆森) sharpened the puzzle in the 1970s and 1980s by adding the footbridge variant, in which the only way to stop the tram is to push a large bystander into its path. The arithmetic is identical — one dies, five live — yet responses flip dramatically. Something other than the count is doing the moral work. Candidate explanations include that pushing uses the person as a means rather than merely foreseeing his death, that it involves personal physical force, and that it violates a distinct constraint against treating a body as an instrument.

The problem has since escaped philosophy entirely. Joshua Greene’s neuroimaging work in the early 2000s found that the footbridge case recruits emotional processing regions more strongly than the switch case, which he read as evidence for a dual-process moral psychology; critics reply that showing which brain regions activate tells you nothing about which answer is correct. Foot herself grew irritated at the industry the case spawned, and philosophers including Barbara Fried have argued the artificial scenarios distort ethics by excluding the risk, uncertainty and institutional context of real decisions. The self-driving car debate has revived it anyway.

🧠 Psychology

The Most Famous Prison Study in Psychology, and the Tapes That Undid It

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In 1971 a Stanford researcher assigned ordinary students at random to be guards or prisoners in a mock jail, and the study was stopped after six days because the guards had turned cruel. For fifty years this was taught as proof that situations overwhelm character. Before reading on, what would you want to check about how that study was actually run before accepting the conclusion?
The plaque marking the basement of Jordan Hall, Stanford University (史丹福大學), where the 1971 study was staged. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

In August 1971 Philip Zimbardo (津巴多) converted a basement corridor at Stanford University (史丹福大學) into a mock prison and randomly assigned twenty-four screened male volunteers to be guards or prisoners. Within days some guards were imposing humiliating punishments, several prisoners had broken down, and the study was terminated after six days instead of two weeks. The conclusion Zimbardo drew, and repeated for decades, was that ordinary people placed in a toxic situation will slide into cruelty — the “situationist” reading. It entered textbooks worldwide and was invoked in commentary on the Abu Ghraib (阿布格萊布) abuses in 2004.

The evidence against that reading came from the study’s own archive. Thibault Le Texier (勒泰西耶), whose 2019 paper in American Psychologist drew on recordings and documents held at Stanford, showed that guards were not left to discover brutality on their own. They were briefed in advance on the atmosphere the researchers wanted, and recordings capture staff coaching a reluctant guard to be tougher and expressing dissatisfaction when guards were too passive. Some prisoner breakdowns were later described by the participants themselves as performance; one has said he faked his most famous outburst partly to get out to study for an exam.

Independent evidence also cuts against the simple version. Reicher and Haslam’s BBC Prison Study (2002) found guards who were given no such steer failed to cohere as a group and did not become tyrannical, suggesting cruelty depends on identification with a leadership that legitimises it, not on the role label alone. None of this means situations do not matter — Milgram’s obedience findings have replicated in modified forms. The lesson is narrower and more useful: a demonstration is not an experiment, and a study with no control condition, a tiny sample and an experimenter who was also the prison superintendent cannot carry the weight placed on it.

🫀 Physiology

Chilli Isn’t Actually Hot — Your Nerves Just Can’t Tell the Difference

🤔 Predict Before You Read
A mouthful of chilli produces burning, sweating, watering eyes and a flushed face — the full physiological signature of contact with something genuinely hot. Yet the chilli is at room temperature and causes no tissue damage at all. Before reading on, what do you think is happening at the level of individual nerve endings that makes your body respond to a chemical as though it were heat?
Capsaicin (辣椒素) in chilli peppers binds the same receptor that detects genuinely painful heat, so the brain receives a burn signal with no burn. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

Pain-sensing nerve endings in the mouth and skin carry a protein called TRPV1 (辣椒素受體), a channel that sits in the nerve membrane and opens when temperature rises above roughly 43 degrees Celsius, the point at which heat becomes damaging. When it opens, ions flood in, the nerve fires, and the brain receives the message “burning.” David Julius (朱利葉斯) and colleagues identified TRPV1 in 1997 by screening for the gene that made cells respond to capsaicin (辣椒素), the active compound in chilli, and Julius shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the work.

The crucial finding was that capsaicin binds TRPV1 directly and opens it at ordinary mouth temperature. The nerve fires exactly the same signal it would send if the tissue were genuinely being cooked, and the brain has no way to distinguish the two, so it launches the full heat-defence response: sweating, vasodilation, tears, and increased salivation. Nothing is being burned. Menthol works as the mirror image through a different channel, TRPM8, which detects cold, which is why mint feels cold in a warm mouth. This is why water helps so little: capsaicin is fat-soluble, so milk, yoghurt or oil strip it away far more effectively.

Two consequences follow. First, birds lack the capsaicin-sensitive form of TRPV1 and eat chillies without discomfort, while mammals avoid them — a plausible evolutionary strategy, since birds disperse the seeds intact while mammalian molars destroy them. Second, repeated capsaicin exposure desensitises the nerve endings, which is why tolerance builds with regular eating and why capsaicin patches are used clinically for neuropathic pain: sustained activation eventually depletes and quietens the fibres. The receptor is real, the danger it reports is not.

💡 Chilli heat is a false alarm on a real alarm system — and milk clears it far better than water.

💊 Health

The 10,000-Step Target Was a Japanese Pedometer Brand Name

🤔 Predict Before You Read
Almost every fitness tracker in the world defaults to a 10,000-step daily goal, and most people assume it emerged from health research. Before reading on, where do you think that specific round number actually came from — and if a large study measured where the mortality benefit of walking actually levels off, would you guess the real figure is higher or lower?
The 10,000-step target originated with a 1960s Japanese pedometer (計步器) brand name, not with clinical research. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

The 10,000-step figure traces to Japan (日本) in the mid-1960s, around the time of the 1964 Tokyo Olympics (東京奧運), when a company marketed a pedometer called the manpo-kei (萬步計), literally “ten-thousand-step meter.” The number was chosen partly because the Japanese character for 10,000, 萬, resembles a walking figure, and partly because it is memorable. It was a product name that happened to become a global health target. No trial established it, and no health authority derived it from outcome data before it spread.

The most cited attempt to test it is Lee et al. (2019) in JAMA Internal Medicine, which tracked step counts in roughly 17,000 older American women and followed mortality over several years. Compared with about 2,700 steps a day, mortality was substantially lower at around 4,400 steps, kept falling as steps increased, and then levelled off at roughly 7,500 steps a day, with no further benefit detected beyond that. Later pooled analyses in younger cohorts have found the plateau sits somewhat higher, in the region of 8,000 to 10,000 steps for adults under sixty, and that stepping intensity matters less than total volume.

Two practical points follow. First, the benefit curve is steepest at the bottom: moving from a sedentary 3,000 steps to 5,000 buys far more health than moving from 9,000 to 11,000, so the person who gains most from walking more is the person currently walking least. Second, the arbitrariness of 10,000 cuts both ways — it is not a medical threshold, so falling short of it is not a failure, but it is also a genuinely useful default because it sits at or above the observed plateau for most age groups. These are observational studies, so reverse causation is a real caveat: people already unwell walk less.

🏋 Sports Science

Why Resting Longer Between Sets Builds More Muscle, Not Less

🤔 Predict Before You Read
For decades the standard advice was that short rests between sets — around sixty seconds — build muscle better, because the metabolic stress and hormone response are greater, while long rests are only for strength athletes. Before reading on, do you think a study that directly compared one minute against three minutes for growth confirmed that, and what mechanism might override the hormone argument?
Rest interval length between sets turned out to matter mainly through its effect on how much total quality work you can complete. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

The short-rest recommendation rested on a real observation: training with brief rests produces a larger acute spike in growth hormone (生長激素) and testosterone (睪固酮) than long rests do. Since these hormones are anabolic, the inference seemed obvious — keep rests to about a minute for hypertrophy (肌肉肥大) and save three minutes for pure strength work. This appeared in textbooks and certification syllabi for years. The weakness was that the inference ran from an acute hormonal marker to a long-term outcome without anyone having measured the long-term outcome directly.

Brad Schoenfeld (舍恩菲爾德) and colleagues did measure it, in a 2016 randomised trial published in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research. Trained men followed identical eight-week programmes differing only in rest length: one minute versus three minutes between sets. The three-minute group gained more muscle thickness and more strength. The mechanism is unglamorous but decisive: with only a minute’s rest, performance on later sets collapses, so a lifter who managed ten repetitions on set one might manage six on set three. The long-rest group simply completed substantially more total work at a meaningful load.

This is a good illustration of a general rule in exercise science: acute hormonal responses to a single session are poor predictors of months of adaptation, and total effective volume is the dominant driver of growth. The practical guidance is to rest around two to three minutes on compound lifts such as squats, deadlifts and presses, where fatigue accumulates fastest, and one to two minutes on isolation work for smaller muscles. Shorter rests are not useless — they are efficient when time is limited, and metabolite-driven training does produce growth — but they are a compromise, not an advantage.

📈 Investing & Economic Thinking

The Convicted Murderer Who Was Handed Control of France’s Entire Economy

🤔 Predict Before You Read
In 1716 a Scottish gambler who had killed a man in a duel and escaped from prison persuaded the French regent to let him found a national bank and issue paper money. Within four years he controlled France’s currency, its national debt and its colonial trade monopoly. Before reading on, what do you think went wrong — and why would a scheme with a genuinely sound core idea still end in collapse?
John Law (約翰·羅), the Scottish financier whose Mississippi scheme (密西西比泡沫) briefly made him the most powerful man in France. Credit: Wikimedia Commons

John Law (約翰·羅) was a Scottish gambler and monetary theorist who fled Britain after killing a man in a duel. His argument, set out in Money and Trade Considered (1705), was genuinely ahead of its time: an economy constrained by the physical supply of gold and silver is starved of the money it needs to trade, and paper currency backed by land or productive assets could expand credit and stimulate output. When Louis XIV died in 1715 leaving France (法國) enormous war debts, the regent, the Duke of Orléans, was desperate enough to let Law try.

Law founded the Banque Générale in 1716, which issued banknotes and was nationalised as the Banque Royale in 1718. Separately he took over the Mississippi Company (密西西比公司), granted a monopoly on trade with France’s Louisiana (路易斯安那) territory, and then absorbed the other trading companies, the tax-collection farms, and the mint. He offered holders of near-worthless government debt the chance to swap it for company shares. The share price rose from around 500 livres in 1719 to roughly 10,000 by December, and the word millionnaire entered use in Paris to describe the newly rich.

The fatal move was linking the two halves. Law had the Banque Royale print notes to lend against share purchases, so the currency’s expansion was collateralised by the shares its own expansion was inflating. Louisiana’s actual trade never came close to justifying the valuations. When large holders began converting shares into gold in early 1720, Law responded by devaluing the notes by decree, which destroyed confidence outright. The system collapsed within months, Law fled France, and the episode left the French state so distrustful of paper money and public banking that it delayed comparable financial institutions for generations — a handicap against Britain that lasted through the century.

⚖️ Political Economics — Theory in the Wild

You Can See the Computer Age Everywhere But in the Productivity Statistics

Corporate computing spread through the 1970s and 1980s, and measured productivity growth slowed. Credit: Wikimedia Commons
🌍 Event
From the 1970s onward, firms across the developed world poured capital into computers and information technology (資訊科技). Over the same period, measured labour productivity growth in the United States (美國) slowed sharply from its post-war pace.
📖 Theory
Growth accounting, developed by Robert Solow (梭羅), attributes output growth to labour, capital and a residual representing technological progress. Investment in more productive capital should show up as faster measured productivity growth.
⚠️ Anomaly
It did not. Solow’s 1987 line — “You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics” — named what became the productivity paradox (生產力悖論). Erik Brynjolfsson (布林約爾松) documented the puzzle formally in 1993. Productivity growth did accelerate briefly from the mid-1990s, then slowed again after 2004 despite smartphones, cloud computing and machine learning.
🧪 Challenger
Paul David (大衛) argued in 1990 that general purpose technologies take decades to pay off, drawing on the electric dynamo: factories had to be physically redesigned before electrification raised output. Brynjolfsson’s later work adds that the complementary intangible capital — process redesign, skills, data — is largely unmeasured, so both the investment and its return are missing from the statistics. Robert Gordon (戈登) counters that recent innovations are simply less economically transformative than plumbing or electricity. Unresolved.
💡 A technology’s payoff shows up only after the organisation around it is rebuilt — which is why the lag looks like failure.
📖 Solow, R. (1987), New York Review of Books. Brynjolfsson, E. (1993), Communications of the ACM. David, P.A. (1990), American Economic Review 80(2). Gordon, R.J. (2016), The Rise and Fall of American Growth.

🔗 Connection of the Day

Cards 6 & 11 — What the Measurement Leaves Out: The Stanford prison study looked like overwhelming evidence because the visible output — cruel guards, broken prisoners — was dramatic, while the coaching that produced it stayed off the record until someone went back to the tapes. The productivity paradox is the same problem inverted: the visible output, measured productivity growth, stayed flat while the thing actually driving value — reorganised processes, accumulated skills, better data — sat outside what national accounts count as investment. In both cases the number everyone argued about was shaped by something systematically excluded from it.

So what: Before accepting a striking result or a disappointing metric, ask what the measurement procedure structurally cannot capture. That question is usually cheaper than more data, and it more often changes the answer.
